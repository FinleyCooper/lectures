  \section{Introduction}
  Recall from IB Quantum Mechanics we have the following:
  \begin{enumerate}
	  \item Wave-particle duality. Waves behaving like particles like light quanta and vice-versa (particles behaving like waves).
	  \item We have a wavefunction $ \psi(x) $ for a particle which we interperate as a probability density $ |\psi(x)|^2 $ (i.e. probability is intristic to the theory).
	  \item Observables become operators on wavefunctions:
		  \begin{itemize}
			  \item $ \hat x \to x $;
			  \item $ \hat p \to -i\hbar \frac\partial{\partial x} $
		  \end{itemize}
		  and the lack of commutation since $ [\hat x, \hat p] =i\hbar  $ leads to the uncertianty relation.
	  \item Schr\"odinger's equation with Hamiltonian $ H $ specifies dynamics and eigenvalues of $ H $ are the allowed energy levels of the of the system.
	  \item All of this explains the hydrogen atom structure and the resulting line spectra.
  \end{enumerate}
  The aims of this course are the following:
  \begin{enumerate}
	  \item Reformulate quantum mechanics in a more powerful, flexible and useful form: the Dirac formulation, which goes beyond wavefunctions. The previous description appears as a particular realisation of our generalisation of quantum states and also leads to alternatives such as
		  \[
			  \tilde \psi(p) \quad \text{with } \hat x \to i\hbar \frac\partial{\partial p} \quad \text{and } \hat p \to p.
		  \]
	  \item This more general approach allows much simpler analysis of known problems (for example the Simple Harmonic Oscillator) but also lots of new things such as \textit{spin} of particles and symmetries (translations, rotations) and the related conservation laws.
	  \item We will also look as a identical particles (bosons and fermions).
	  \item We will develop a framework for quantising more general systems such as EM fields.
	  \item We will also setup perturbation theory which is a practical tool for computation but we will not heavily consider the detailed applications.
  \end{enumerate}
  \section{Dirac Formalism}
  \subsection{Concepts and Definitions}
  A quantum system is described at each instant by a \textit{state} $ |\psi \rangle $ which belons to a complex vector space $ V $; any non-zero element of this space $ V $ is a possible state. Thus
  \[
	  \mid \psi\rangle,\; \mid \phi\rangle \in V\implies \alpha \mid \psi\rangle + \beta\mid \phi\rangle \in V  \text{ for any } \alpha,\beta\in \C.
  \]
  Physically rhis is a superposition principle leading to wave-like behaviour but these states are \textit{not wavefunctions}. There are also \textit{dual} of conjugate states $ \langle \psi \mid $ which belong to the dual space $ V^\dagger $. By definition states and duals can be combined or paired to give a complex number. The map
  \[
	  \ff{\psi \mid , \mid \psi} \to \ff{\psi \mid \phi}
  \]
defines a map from $ V^\dagger \times V \to \C $ with
\[
\ff{\phi \mid (\alpha_1 \mid \psi_1\rangle + \alpha_2 \mid \psi_2\rangle ) = \alpha_1 \ff{\phi\mid \psi_1 } + \alpha_2 \langle \phi \mid \psi_2\rangle
\]
and
\[
	\langle ( \beta_1\langle \phi_1 \mid + \beta_2 \langle \phi_2 \mid ) \mid \psi\rangle = \beta_1 \ff{\phi_1\mid\psi} + \beta_2 \ff{\phi_2\mid \psi}.
\]
The spaces $ V $ and $ V^\dagger $ comes with an inner product which can be described as a one-to-one correspondence between the spaces
\begin{align*}
	V&\to V^\dagger\\
	\mid \psi \rangle & \to \langle \psi \mid = \left(\mid\psi\rangle\right)^\dagger.
\end{align*}
We also have that if we take a linear combination of states to the dual space, the coefficients have to have their complex conjugates taken to from the dual space to $ V $ or visa-versa. Hence the inner product is
\[
	\mid\phi\rangle, \mid\psi\rangle \to \ff{ \phi\mid \psi} = (\mid \psi\rangle)^\dagger \mid \psi\rangle
\]
and it is assumed to obey
\[
	\ff {\phi\mid\psi} = \ff {\psi\mid\phi}^*
\]
since the inner product is hermitian, and is the inner product defines a positive definite norm on $ V $.
\[
  ||\mid\psi\rangle||^2 = \langle \psi \mid \psi\rangle.
\]
\begin{remark}
	Konwing $ \ff {\phi\mid \psi} $ for all $ \langle \phi\mid $ determines $ \mid\psi\rangle $ and visa-versa.
\end{remark}
We can show this by
\[
	\ff {\phi\mid \psi_1} = \ff{\phi\mid \psi_2} \implies \ff{\psi\mid \chi} = 0 
\]
where $ \mid\chi\rangle = \mid\psi_1\rangle - \mid\psi_2\rangle $. If this holds for all $ \langle \phi\mid $ we can set $ \langle \phi\mid = \langle \chi \mid $ so by positve definiteness we have that the norm of $ \mid\chi\rangle $ is zero, so $ \mid\chi\rangle=0 $ too.
\par
We also have that the physically content of any state is unchanged by
\[
  \mid\psi\rangle \to \alpha\mid\psi\rangle
\]
for any $ \alpha\in \C $ non-zero. So we will usually normalise to make the state have norm $ 1 $, but we can still change by a phase vector.
\begin{remark}
  Relative phases can be important in expressions where we're adding a linear combination of elements of $ V $. We also assume the space $ V $ is complete so appropriate sequences and series converge in $ V $.
\end{remark}
A complete inner product space is called a \textit{Hilbert space}.
\par
\begin{definition}
	(Operator) An \textit{operator} \theta is a linear map on states
	\[
	  \mid \psi \rangle \to Q \langle \psi \rangle.
	\]
\end{definition}
The same operator can be thought of as acting to the left on dual states, so
\[
  \langle \phi \mid \to \langle \phi \mid Q
\]
where the right hand side is defined by
\[
  \left(\langle \phi \mid Q \right)\mid \psi\rangle = \langle \phi\mid \left(Q\mid \psi\rangle\right) = \langle \phi\mid Q\mid \psi\rangle
\]

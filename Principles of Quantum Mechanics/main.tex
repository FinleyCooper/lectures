\section{Introduction}

Recall from IB Quantum Mechanics we have the following:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item Wave-particle duality. Waves behave like particles, like light quanta, and vice versa (particles behave like waves).

    \item We have a wavefunction $\psi(x)$ for a particle which we interpret as a probability density $|\psi(x)|^2$ (i.e. probability is intrinsic to the theory).

    \item Observables become operators on wavefunctions:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item $\hat{x} \to x$;
            \item $\hat{p} \to -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$
        \end{itemize}

        The lack of commutation, since $[\hat{x},\hat{p}] = i\hbar$, leads to the uncertainty relation.

    \item Schrödinger's equation with Hamiltonian $H$ specifies dynamics, and eigenvalues of $H$ are the allowed energy levels of the system.

    \item All of this explains the hydrogen atom structure and the resulting line spectra.

\end{enumerate}

The aims of this course are the following:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item Reformulate quantum mechanics in a more powerful, flexible and useful form: the Dirac formulation, which goes beyond wavefunctions. The previous description appears as a particular realisation of our generalisation of quantum states and also leads to alternatives such as

        \[
            \tilde{\psi}(p)
            \quad \text{with} \quad
            \hat{x} \to i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial p}
            \quad \text{and} \quad
            \hat{p} \to p.
        \]

    \item This more general approach allows much simpler analysis of known problems (for example, the Simple Harmonic Oscillator), but also lots of new things such as \textit{spin} of particles and symmetries (translations, rotations) and the related conservation laws.

    \item We will also look at identical particles (bosons and fermions).

    \item We will develop a framework for quantising more general systems such as EM fields.

    \item We will also set up perturbation theory, which is a practical tool for computation, but we will not heavily consider the detailed applications.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Dirac Formalism}

\subsection{Concepts and Definitions}

A quantum system is described at each instant by a \textit{state} $\ket{\psi}$ which belongs to a complex vector space $V$; any non-zero element of this space $V$ is a possible state. Thus

\[
    \ket{\psi}, \ket{\phi} \in V
    \implies
    \alpha\ket{\psi} + \beta\ket{\phi} \in V
    \quad \text{for any } \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}.
\]

Physically, this is a superposition principle leading to wave-like behaviour, but these states are \textit{not wavefunctions}. There are also \textit{dual} or conjugate states $\bra{\psi}$ which belong to the dual space $V^\dagger$. By definition, states and duals can be combined or paired to give a complex number. The map

\[
    (\bra{\psi}, \ket{\phi}) \mapsto \bra{\psi}\ket{\phi}
\]

defines a map from $V^\dagger \times V \to \mathbb{C}$ with

\[
    \bra{\phi}
    \left(\alpha_1\ket{\psi_1}+\alpha_2\ket{\psi_2}\right)
    =
    \alpha_1\bra{\phi}\ket{\psi_1}
    +
    \alpha_2\bra{\phi}\ket{\psi_2}
\]

and

\[
    \left(\beta_1\bra{\phi_1}+\beta_2\bra{\phi_2}\right)\ket{\psi}
    =
    \beta_1\bra{\phi_1}\ket{\psi}
    +
    \beta_2\bra{\phi_2}\ket{\psi}.
\]

The spaces $V$ and $V^\dagger$ come with an inner product which can be described as a one-to-one correspondence between the spaces

\begin{align*}
    V &\to V^\dagger \\
    \ket{\psi} &\to \bra{\psi}
    = \left(\ket{\psi}\right)^\dagger.
\end{align*}

We also have that if we take a linear combination of states to the dual space, the coefficients have to have their complex conjugates taken when mapping from the dual space to $V$ or vice versa. Hence the inner product is

\[
    \ket{\phi},\ket{\psi}
    \mapsto
    \bra{\phi}\ket{\psi}
    =
    \left(\ket{\phi}\right)^\dagger\ket{\psi}
\]

and it is assumed to obey

\[
    \bra{\phi}\ket{\psi}
    =
    \left(\bra{\psi}\ket{\phi}\right)^*
\]

since the inner product is Hermitian, and the inner product defines a positive-definite norm on $V$:

\[
    \|\ket{\psi}\|^2 = \bra{\psi}\ket{\psi}.
\]

\begin{remark}

    Knowing $\bra{\phi}\ket{\psi}$ for all $\bra{\phi}$ determines $\ket{\psi}$ and vice versa.

\end{remark}

We can show this by

\[
    \bra{\phi}\ket{\psi_1}
    =
    \bra{\phi}\ket{\psi_2}
    \implies
    \bra{\phi}\ket{\chi}=0,
\]

where $\ket{\chi}=\ket{\psi_1}-\ket{\psi_2}$. If this holds for all $\bra{\phi}$, we can set $\bra{\phi}=\bra{\chi}$, so by positive definiteness we have that the norm of $\ket{\chi}$ is zero, and therefore $\ket{\chi}=0$ too.

\par

We also have that the physical content of any state is unchanged by

\[
    \ket{\psi} \to \alpha\ket{\psi}
\]

for any non-zero $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$. So we will usually normalise to make the state have norm $1$, but we can still change it by a phase factor.

\begin{remark}

    Relative phases can be important in expressions where we're adding a linear combination of elements of $V$. We also assume the space $V$ is complete so appropriate sequences and series converge in $V$.

\end{remark}

A complete inner product space is called a \textit{Hilbert space}.

\par

\begin{definition}

    (Operator) An \textit{operator} $Q$ is a linear map on states

    \[
        \ket{\psi} \to Q\ket{\psi}.
    \]

\end{definition}

The same operator can be thought of as acting to the left on dual states, so

\[
    \bra{\phi} \to \bra{\phi}Q
\]

where the right-hand side is defined by

\[
    \left(\bra{\phi}Q\right)\ket{\psi}
    =
    \bra{\phi}\left(Q\ket{\psi}\right)
    =
    \bra{\phi}Q\ket{\psi}.
\]
\begin{remark}
	States and operators $ \ket \psi, \bra \phi, Q $ can be dealt with by rulers that mirror those for complex vectors $ \mathbf u (\text{col}), \mathbf v^\dagger(\text{row}) $ and a matrix $ A $. So we can compare $ \bra \psi Q \ket \phi $ with $ \mathbf{v}^\dagger A\mathbf u $ like we do with inner products.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
	(Eigenstate) For any operator $ Q $ with say that $ \ket \psi $ non-zero is an \textit{eigenstate} with \textit{eigenvalue} $ \lambda $ if 
	\[
	  Q\ket \psi = \lambda \ket \psi
	\]
	or equivalently
	\[
	  \bra \psi Q^\dagger = \lambda^*\bra \psi.
	\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
  For a general $ Q $, $ \lambda $ may be complex.
\end{remark}
\begin{definition}
	(Hermitian Conjugate) For any operator $ Q $, the \textit{Hermitian conjugate} or \textit{adjoint map} $ Q^\dagger $ is an operator defined by
	\[
	  \bra \psi Q^\dagger = (Q\ket \psi)^\dagger
	\]
	for any $ \ket \psi $.
\end{definition}
Equivalently
\begin{align*}
	\bra \phi Q^\dagger \ket \psi &= (Q\bra \phi)^\dagger \ket \psi \\
				      &= \bra \psi Q \ket \phi^\dagger
\end{align*}
for all $ \ket\psi,\ket \phi $.
\par
\begin{definition}
	(Hermitian) An operator $ Q $ is \textit{Hermitian} or \textit{self-adjoint} if
	\[
		Q^\dagger = Q
	\]
	and such operators are called \textit{observables} because they correspond to physical quantities that can be measured, for example position, momentum, energy.
\end{definition}
To define a quantum system, we must specify the \textit{Hamiltonian} whose eigenvalues are the allowed energy levels of the system,
\[
  H\ket \chi = E\ket \chi.
\]
We have the following key results for any Hermitian operator $ Q $.
\begin{proposition}
  For a Hermitian operator $ Q $,
  \begin{enumerate}
	  \item All eigenvalues are real;
	  \item Eigenstates with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal;
	  \item Any state can be written as a linear combination of its eigenstates (i.e its matrix representation is diagonalisable).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
  For a proof of (iii) we have a proof in IB Linear Algebra. We'll cover a proof of (i) and (ii) here.\par
  \pf Suppose that $ Q\ket \psi = \lambda\ket \psi $. So taking Hermitian conjugates on both sides we have that 
  \begin{align*}
	  \bra \psi Q^\dagger &= \lambda^*\bra \psi \\
			      &= \bra \psi Q.
  \end{align*}
  Hence $ \bra \psi Q \ket \psi = \lambda \bra{\psi}\ket{\psi} = \lambda^* \bra{\psi}\ket{\psi} $. Since $ \ket \psi $ is non-zero, it's norm is non-zero hence $ \lambda = \lambda^* $.

\par
For the second part let $ \ket n $ be an eigenstate of $ Q $ with eigenvalues $ \lambda = q_n $ real. Then we have that
\begin{align*}
	Q \ket n &= q_n\ket n \\
	Q\ket m &= q_m\ket m \\
	\bra m Q&= q_m\bra m
\end{align*}
hence $ \bra m Q \ket n = q_n\bra{m}\ket{n} = q_m\bra{m}\ket{n} $. But we have that $ q_m\ne q_n $ so $ \bra{m}\ket{n} = 0 $.\qed
\subsection{The Harmonic Oscillator}
\subsubsection{Construction of the eigenstates}
A one-dimensional harmonic oscillator of mass $ m $ and frequency $ \omega $ is a system with position $ \hat x $, momentum $ \hat p $ and Hamilitonian
\[
	 H = \frac 1{2m} \hat p^2 + \frac  12 m \omega^2 \hat x^2.
\]
We seek the energy eigenstates and eigenvalues of $ H $. We do this by introducting \textit{annihilation, creation, and number operators}.\par
Define
\begin{align*}
	a &= \left(\frac {m\omega}{2\hbar}\right)^{\frac 12}\left(\hat x + \frac{i\hat  p }{m \omega}\right) \\
	a^\dagger &= \left(\frac {m\omega}{2\hbar}\right)^{\frac 12}\left(\hat x - \frac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right).
	\end{align*}
Equivalently 
\begin{align*}
	\hat x &= \left(\frac \hbar{2 m \omega}\right)^{\frac 12} \left( a + a^\dagger\right) \\
   \hat p &= \left(\frac {\hbar m \omega}2\right)^{\frac 12}i\left( a^\dagger - a\right).
\end{align*}
It's easy to check that
\[
	[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar \iff [a, a^\dagger] = 1.
\]
In fact,
\begin{align*}
	aa^\dagger  &= \frac{m\omega}{2\hbar} \hat x^2 + \frac 1{2m\omega \hbar} \hat p ^2 - \frac i{2\hbar} (\hat x \hat p - \hat p \hat x) \\
	&= \frac 1{\hbar \omega} H + \frac 12
\end{align*}
but
\[
	a^\dagger a = \frac 1{\hbar \omega} H - \frac 12.
\]
This confirms the commutation relation, but we also find that
\begin{align*}
  H = \hbar \omega \left(N + \frac 12\right)
\end{align*}
with $ N = a^\dagger a $, the \textit{number operator} which is clearly Hermitian, so finding eigenvalues and eigenstates of $ N $ is equivalent to finding these for $ H $.\par
Let $ \ket \lambda $ be any eigenstate of $ N $ with eigenvalue $ \lambda $, so
\[
  N \ket \lambda = \lambda \ket \lambda \iff H \ket \lambda = E \ket \lambda
\]
where $ E = \hbar \omega\left(\lambda + \frac 12 \right) $. Then assuming that $ \ket \lambda $ is normalised, write
\[
	\lambda = \bra\lambda N \ket \lambda = \lambda a^\dagger a \ket \lambda = (a\ket\lambda)^\dagger (a\ket \lambda) = ||a\ket \lambda||^2
\]
we could have just seen this directly, since $ N $ is clearly positive semi-definite from its construction as the outer product of an operator. So we have that $ \lambda \ge 0  $ and $ \lambda = 0 \iff a\ket \lambda = 0 $.\par 
Now we consider
\begin{align*}
	N(a^\dagger \ket \lambda) & = ([N, a^\dagger] + a^\dagger N)\ket \lambda \\
				  &= ( a^\dagger + a^\dagger \lambda) \ket \lambda \\
				  &= (\lambda + 1) (a^\dagger \ket \lambda).
\end{align*}
Similarly we have that
\begin{align*}
	N(a\ket \lambda) = &= ([N,a] + aN)\ket \lambda\\
			   &= ( -a + a\lambda)\ket\lambda \\
			   &= (\lambda -1 ) a \ket \lambda.
\end{align*}
Here we have used the results for commutators $ [N,a^\dagger] = [a^\dagger, a^\dagger] = a^\dagger[a,a^\dagger] = a^\dagger $ and similarily $ [N, a] = -a $.
\par
Now the news states with be eigenstates provided their norms are non-zero. We can compute 
\begin{align*}
	||a\ket \lambda ||^2 &= \lambda\\
	||a^\dagger \ket \lambda ||^2 &= \bra \lambda aa^\dagger \ket \lambda = \lambda a^\dagger a + 1 \ket \lambda \\
			     &= \lambda + 1.
\end{align*}
Because of these properties $ a^\dagger $ and $ a $ are called \textit{creation} (or raising) and \textit{annihilation} (or lowering) operators.\par
We can't lower below zero, since the eigenvalues are positive so the eigenvalues must be a subset of the positive integers to avoid a contradiction. We can lower and raise all we want, so we can generate any other state with eigenvalues being all of the non-negative integers.\par
More formally, since we know that $ \lambda \ge 0  $, so for a given eigenstate $ \ket \lambda $, if $ \lambda $ is \textit{not} an integer, then
\[
  a\ket \lambda, a^2\ket\lambda, \dots, a^n\ket \lambda
\]
are states with eigenvalues
\[
  \lambda -1, \lambda -2, \dots, \lambda -n
\]
but for $ n $ sufficiently large $ \lambda - n <0 $ which is a contradiction.
\par
By contrast if $ \lambda =k \in \N $ then the contradiction is avoided because
\[
  a\ket k, a^2 \ket k, \dots, a^k \ket k
\]
with eigenvalues
\[
  k-1, k-2, \dots, 0
\]
so we can't lower anymore because our eigenstate has collapsed to zero, i.e. if we force integer eigenvalues the resultant eigenstate is \textit{nilpotent}.\par
We can use the raising operator $ a^\dagger $ to generate the eigenstates with eigenvalues $ k+1, k+2, \dots $ forever.
\par
Hence the eigenvalues of $ N $ are therefore precisely the non-negative integers and the oscillator has energy levels
\[
  E_n = \hbar \omega\left(n+\frac 12 \right).
\]
From our calculation of norms above, we can choose normalised eigenstates $ \ket n $ related by 
\begin{align*}
	a^\dagger \ket n &= \sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1} \\
	a\ket n &= \sqrt{n} \ket{n-1}.
\end{align*}
Starting from any state, $ \ket n $, we can generate all others. In particular, starting from the \textit{ground state} $ \ket 0 $, characteristed by 
\[
  a\ket 0 = 0
\]
we can construct normalised eigenstates
\[
	\ket n =\frac 1{\sqrt{n!}} (a^\dagger)^n \ket 0.
\]
\begin{remark}
	Beware that $ \ket 0 $ is \textit{not} zero in $ V $. It is an eigenstate and therefore non-zero by definition.
\end{remark}
\subsubsection{Importance of the oscillator}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item A smooth potential $ V(x) $ with equilibrium point $ x = x_0 $ (so that $ V'(x_0) = 0 $) can be expanded by
		\[
		  V(x_0 + x) = V(x_0) + \frac 12 V''(x_0) x^2 + O(x^3)
		\]
		so, if $ x=x_0 $ is \textit{stable}, then we can set $ V''(x_0) = m\omega^2 > 0 $ and for displacements $ x $ not too large, we can expect the system to be well approximated by a oscillator.
	\item This can also be applied in the case for a diatomic molecules where we have quantisation of vibrational energies which is important in understanding the internal energy and heat capacities which result in macroscopic consequences.
	\item More complicated systems can be analysed in terms of normal modes. Coherent motion in which all degrees of freedom oscillate with the same common frequency $ \omega $. The general solution for a classical oscillator is
		\[
			x(t) = A e^{-i\omega t} + A^* e^{i\omega t}
		\]
		where $ A $ is a complex constant.\par
		Normal modes for a system with variables $ x_N $ are solutions of the form
		\[
			x_N(t) = Au_Ne^{-i\omega t} + A^* u^*_N e^{i\omega t}
		\]
		with $ u_N $ a complex vector specific to each normal frequency $ \omega $.
	\item For example consider a crystal with a number of atoms $ \mathcal N \sim 10^{23} $ where the force are approximately elastic and $ N $ labels diretions of motion for each atom, so we have $ 3\times \mathcal N $ values.
	\item Or we consider an EM field, where normal modes are oscillations of electric and mangetic at each point in space are given by
		\[
			\mathbf E(\x,t) = A\mathbf u(\x) e^{-i\omega t} + A^*\mathbf u(\x)^*e^{i\omega t}.
		\]
		This is exact.
	\item Normal modes are independent oscillator: treat these as above. For each mode we have
		\[
			a(\omega) \quad\text{and}\quad a(\omega)^\dagger
		\]
		where are ground state $ \ket 0 $ with $ a(\omega)\ket 0  = 0$. Non-ground states created with the raising operators add energy and momentum and behave like particles. In this way we can see that $ a $ destroys particles and $ a^\dagger $ creates particles. 
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Bases and Measurements}
\subsubsection{Bases of Eigenstates}
We know that for any observable $ Q $ there is an orthonormal basis of eigenstates $ \{\ket n\} $ for $ V $,
\[
  Q \ket n = q_n\ket n.
\]
We have orthonormality $ \bra{m}\ket{n} = \delta_{mn} $ and for any state $ \ket \psi \in V $ we have that
\[
  \ket \psi = \sum_n \alpha_n \ket n
\]
where $ \alpha_n = \bra{n}\ket{\psi} $. Note that $ ||\ket \psi||^2 = 1 $ if and only if
\[
	\bra{\psi}\ket{\psi} = \left(\sum_m \alpha_m^*\bra m\right)\left(\sum_n\alpha_n \ket n \right) = \sum_n |\alpha_n|^2 = 1.
\]
Note that we can write
\[
  1 = \sum_n \ket n \bra n.
\]
since for any state $ \ket \psi $ we have that
\begin{align*}
	\ket \psi &= \left(\sum_n \ket n \bra n\right)\ket \psi \\
		  &= \sum_n \ket n(\bra{n}\ket{\psi})
\end{align*}
In above there may be several independent staets with the same eigenvalue $ \lambda $. The eigenspace for a given eigenvalue is
\[
	V_\lambda = \{ \ket \psi : Q\ket \psi = \lambda \ket \psi\}.
\]
Note that $ V_\lambda $ has basis $ \{ \ket n : q_n = \lambda\} $. The \textit{degeneracy} of $ \lambda $ is the number of sattes in this basis (i.e the dimension of $ V_\lambda $). If $ V_\lambda $ has dimension $ 1 $, then we say that $ \lambda $ is non-degenerate.
\subsubsection{Functions of Operators}
It is straight forward to define $ f(Q) $ for some operator $ Q $ if $ f $ is a polynmoial or power series (assuming convergence). For example we can consider $ f(Q) = Q^3 + Q $ or $ f(Q) = e^Q $ in this way. Important examples are $ e^{-iHt/\hbar} $ which is the time-evolution operator and $ e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar} $ which is the translation operator through some fixed displacement $ a $ in the $ x $ direction.\par
More generally, for $ Q $ an observable, with basis of eigenstates, we can define
\[
  f(Q) \ket n = f(q_n) \ket n
\]
and then defined on any state, provided that $ f(q_n) $ makes sense. For example for $ f(Q) = \frac 1Q $, we have that
\[
	\frac 1 Q \ket n = \frac 1 {q_n} \ket n
\]
for $ q_n\ne 0 $.
\par
In addition we have that 
\begin{align*}
	Q &= \sum_n q_n \ket n \bra n \\
	f(Q) &= \sum_n f(q_n) \ket n \bra n.
\end{align*}
Recall our eigenspace $ V_\lambda $ and consider a projection operation $ P_\lambda $ onto this subspace, $ V\to V_\lambda $ can be defined by its action on basis states
\[
  P_\lambda \ket n = \begin{cases}
	  \ket n & q_n = \lambda \\
	  0 & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
\]
which can be written as
\[
	P_\lambda  = \sum_{n: \ q_n = \lambda} \ket n \bra n.
\]
\subsubsection{Measurement Axioms}
Consider a measurement of $ Q $ (observable) when the system is in a normalised state $ \ket \psi $. Then
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The result in an eigenvalue $ \lambda $ of $ Q $;
	\item This result is obtained with probability
		\[
			p(\lambda) = ||P_\lambda \ket \psi ||^2 = \sum_{n:\ q_n=\lambda} |\alpha_n|^2.
		\]
	\item Immediately after the measurement the state is
		\[
			c P_\lambda \ket \psi = c\sum_{n:\ q_n=\lambda} \alpha_n \ket n
		\]
		where $ c $ is a constant for normalisation. 
\end{enumerate}
It is often the case with $ \lambda $ being a non-generate eigenstate. In that case we just get that $ p(\lambda) = |\bra{n}\ket{\psi}|^2 = |\alpha_n|^2 $ where $ \alpha_n $ is often called the amplitude of the state.
\par
We have that 
\[
	\sum_{\lambda} p(\lambda) = 1.
\]
\begin{example}
  Consider a system with space $ V $ with orthonormal basis states
  \[
    \ket 1, \ket 2, \ket 3
  \]
  which are eigenstaets of $ Q $ with eigenvalues $ q_1=q_2 = 0 $ and $ q_3 = 1 $. Consider
  \[
	  \ket \psi = \frac 1{\sqrt 6}\left(2 \ket 1 + \ket 2 + \ket 3\right)
  \]
  So we get that
  \begin{align*}
	  p(1) &= \left|\frac 1{\sqrt 6}\right|^2 & = \frac 16 \\
	  p(0) &= \left|\frac 2{\sqrt 6}\right|^2 + \left|\frac 1{\sqrt 6}\right|^2 = \frac 56
  \end{align*}
  and the state afterwards is $ \ket 3 $ if $ 1 $ was measured and is 
  \[
  \frac 1{\sqrt 5}(2 \ket 1 + \ket 2)  
  \]
  if we measured $ 0 $.
\end{example}
\begin{definition}
	(Expectation value) The \textit{expectation value} or \textit{mean} of $ Q $ in a state $ \ket \psi $ is
	\[
	  \langle Q\rangle_\psi = \bra \psi Q \ket \psi = \sum_\lambda \lambda p(\lambda) = \sum_n q_n|\alpha_n|^2. 
	\]
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
	(Uncertainty) The \textit{uncertainty} or \textit{variance} of an operator $ Q $ in a state $ \ket \psi $ is
	\[
	  \left(\Delta Q\right)^2_\psi = \langle (Q-\langle Q\rangle_\psi)^2\rangle_\psi = \langle Q^2 \rangle_\psi - \langle Q\rangle^2_\psi.
	\]
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
  In thinking about many repeated measurements, we must prepare the system in the same state $ \ket \psi $ each time.
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}
  If $ \ket \psi $ is an eigenstate of $ Q $ with eigenvalue $ \lambda $ then
  \[
	  \langle Q \rangle_\psi = \lambda \quad \text{and}\quad (\Delta Q)_\psi = 0.
  \]
  Quantum mechanical behaviour arises from the fact that certain obserables do not commute. In general, in any state $ \ket \psi $
  \[
	  (\Delta A)_\psi(\Delta B)_\psi \ge \frac 12 \left|\langle [A,B]\rangle_\psi\right|
  \]
  so $ [A,B]\ne 0 $ implies that we cannot expect to measure $ A $ and $ B $ at the same time.\par

  \textit{A proof of the general uncertainty relation can be seen from expanding} $ ||(A+i\lambda B)\ket \psi||^2 \ge 0 $.
\end{remark}
The prototypical case is with position and momentum $ \hat x, \hat p $ which obey
\[
	[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar 
\]
in one dimension and $ \Delta x \Delta p \ge \frac \hbar2 $.\par
In three dimensions we have 
\[
	[\hat x_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar \delta_{ij}
\]
and the uncertainty applies to position and momentum in a particular direction.
\subsubsection{Simultaneous measurement and complete commuting sets}
If an observable $ Q $ does not distinguish states completely because some eigenvalues are degenerate, then we can look for an additional obserable $ Q' $ such that $ Q,Q' $ are simultaneously measureable (i.e simultaneously diagonalisable) and preserves the eigenspaces of the other operator. This means we have joint eigenstates $ \ket {\lambda,\lambda'} $ with
\begin{align*}
	Q \ket {\lambda, \lambda'} &= \lambda \ket{\lambda, \lambda'} \\
	Q' \ket{\lambda,\lambda'} &= \lambda' \ket{\lambda,\lambda'}
\end{align*}
so $ [Q,Q'] = 0 $. Conversely if $ [Q,Q'] = 0 $, it is easy to check that $ Q':V_\lambda \to V_\lambda $ and hence we can find a basis of eigenstates of $ Q' $ in $ V_\lambda $ which gives us joint eigenstates for $ Q $ and $ Q' $. Since we assume that $ Q $ is Hermitian so $ V $ is a direct sum of eigenstates, we can do this for all eigenstates, giving us a complete basis of joint eigenstates, which we call $ \ket{\lambda,\lambda'} $.
\begin{definition}
	(Complete commuting set) A set of observables $ \{Q,Q',Q'',\dots \} $ is a \textit{complete commuting set} if their pairwise commute. This implies that there is a basis of joint eigenstates, and the set of eigenvalues $ \lambda,\lambda',\lambda'',\dots $ label states uniquely (up to normalisation)
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Equivalently if $ A $ is any observable such that $ [A,Q]=[A,Q']=\dots = 0 $ then $ A = f(Q,Q',\dots) $.
\end{remark}
\begin{example}
	Consider the previous example with orthonormal satets $ \ket 1, \ket 2, \ket 3 $ and eigenstates of $ Q $ with $ \lambda = 0 $ for $ \ket 1 $ and $ \ket 2 $ and $ \lambda = 1 $ for $ \ket 3 $. Now consider a second observabke $ Q' $ with $ Q'\ket 1 = \ket 2, Q'\ket 2 = \ket 1, Q'\ket 3 = 0 $. Now by writing a general vector in the orthonormal basis, we have that $ [Q,Q'] = 0 $. Now let 
	\[
		\ket \pm = \frac 1{\sqrt 2}(\ket 1 \pm \ket 2)
	\]
	so then
	\[
	  Q' \ket \pm = \pm \ket \pm
	\]
	$ Q,Q' $ is a complete commuting set with basis of joint eigenstates $ \ket +, \ket -, \ket 3 $.
\end{example}
\subsection{Bases, Representations and Wavefunctions}
\subsubsection{Discrete and Continuous Bases}
Consider orthonormal basis $ \{\ket n\} $ with $ n $ discrete label. We can regard all states and operators as row or column vectors and matrices respectively
\begin{align*}
	\ket{\psi} &= \sum_n \alpha_n \ket{n} \iff \alpha_n &= \bra{n}\ket{\psi} \\
	\ket{\phi} &= \sum_n \beta_n\ket{n} \iff \beta_n &= \bra{n}\ket{\phi} \\
\end{align*}
and inner product
\[
	\bra{\phi}\ket{\psi} = \sum_n \beta_n^*\alpha_n.
\]
For an operator $ A $,
\[
	A\ket n = \sum_m \ket m A_{mn}
\]
where $ A_{mn} $ is a complex matrix with entries $ \bra{m}A\ket{n} $.\par
It's simple to check that
\[
	\ket \phi = A \ket \psi \iff \beta_{m} = \sum_n A_{mn} \alpha_n
\]
and also if $ B $ is another operator with matrix $ B_{mn} = \bra m B\ket n $ then
\[
	(AB)_{mn} = \sum_p A_{mp}B_{pn}
\]
i.e matrix multiplication.\par
The choice of basis provides a representation of the quantum states and operators. We can also allow the index $ n $ to be continuous. This requires modifications such as the following:
\begin{align*}
	\sum_n  &\longrightarrow \int \mathrm dn \\
	\bra {n}\ket {m} = \delta_{nm} &\longrightarrow \delta(n-m) \\
	\ket \psi = \sum_n \alpha\ket n &\longrightarrow \int \mathrm dn \ \alpha_n \ket n \\
\end{align*}
and the completeness relation goes to
\[
  I = \sum_n \ket n \bra n \longrightarrow \int \mathrm dn \ket n \bra n.
\]
Note that if basis a bove arise as eigenstates of an obserable then
\[
	|\alpha_n|^2 = |\bra{n}\ket{\psi}|^2 \longrightarrow \text{probability density for continuous range } n.
\]
\subsubsection{Position and Momentum Bases}
Considera particle in one dimension with position $ \hat x $ and momentum $ \ket p $ obeying the relation $ [\hat x,\hat p] = i \hbar $. Let $ \ket x $ be the position eigenstates
\[
  \hat x \ket x = x \ket x
\]
which are continuous eigenvalues with
\[
	\bra{x} \ket{x'} = \delta(x-x'), \quad \int\mathrm dx \ket x \bra x = I.
\]
Any state can be written as
\[
  \ket\psi = \int \mathrm dx \ \psi(x) \ket x 
\]
with $ \psi(x) = \bra{x}\ket{\psi} $ and this is the usual position space wavefunction. From our remarks in the previous section $ |\psi(x)|^2 $ is a probability density for measuring position.\par
Our inner product of two wavefunctions from Part IB Quantum mechanics is recovered through
\begin{align*}
	\bra {\phi}\ket{\psi} &= \left(\int \mathrm dx\ \phi(x)^*\bra x\right)\left(\int \mathrm dx' \ \psi(x') \ket x'\right) \\
			      &= \int \mathrm dx \int\mathrm dx' \ \phi(x)^* \psi(x') \bra{x}\ket{x'} = \int\mathrm dx \ \phi(x)^*\psi(x) \\
\end{align*}
and
\[
	||\ket \psi||^2 = \bra{\psi}\ket{\psi} = \int\mathrm dx |\psi(x)|^2 = 1
\]
if normalised.\par
Similarly, define momentum eigenstate $ \ket p $ with
\begin{align*}
  \hat p \ket p = p \ket p
\end{align*}
and
\[
	\bra{p}\ket{p'} = \delta(p-p'),\quad \int\mathrm dp \ket p \bra p = I.
\]
Now we can expand
\begin{align*}
	\ket \psi &= \int \mathrm dp \ \tilde \psi(p) \ket p 
\end{align*}
with $ \tilde \psi(p) = \bra{p}\ket{\psi} $ momentum space wavefunction and $ |\tilde \psi(p)|^2 $ probability distribution for measurements of momentum.\par
To proceed, we need the following result that
\begin{align*}
	\bra{x}\ket{p} &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}} e^{ipx/\hbar} \\
	\bra{p}\ket{x} &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}} e^{-ipx/\hbar} \\
\end{align*}
which we will assume holds. Consider the action of $ \hat x $ and $ \hat p $ on position space wavefunctions. So suppose we have $ \hat x \ket \psi $ has wavefunction
\[
	\bra x \hat x \ket \psi = x\bra {x}\ket {\psi} = x \psi(x).
\]
Similarly if we have $ \hat p \ket \psi $, the wavefunction is
\begin{align*}
	\bra x \hat p \ket \psi &= \int\mathrm dp \bra x \hat p \ket p \bra{p}\ket{\psi} \\
				&=\int \mathrm dp\ p \bra {x}\ket{p} \bra{p}\ket{\psi}
\end{align*}
and note that $ p\bra{x}\ket{p} = -i\hbar \frac\partial{\partial x} \bra {x}\ket{p} $, so hence
\begin{align*}
	\bra x \hat p \ket \psi& = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \int \mathrm dp \ \bra {x}\ket{p} \bra{p} \ket{\psi}\\
			       &= -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bra{x}\ket{\psi} \\
			       &= -i\hbar \psi'(x).
\end{align*}
which gives us the action of $ \hat x $ and $ \hat p $ on position space wavefunctions. Similarly on momentum space wavefunctions we calculate $ \bra p \hat x \ket \psi $ and $ \bra p \hat p \ket \psi $ and the result is that
\begin{align*}
	\hat x &\longrightarrow i\hbar \frac\partial{\partial p} \\
	\hat p &\longrightarrow p. \\
\end{align*}
The relationship between $ \psi(x) $ and $ \tilde\psi(p) $ also follows from our assumed result, as
\begin{align*}
	\tilde \psi(p) = \bra{p}\ket{\psi} &= \int \mathrm dx \bra{p}\ket{x} \bra{x}\ket{\psi} \\
					   &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}} \int\mathrm dx \ e^{-ipx/\hbar} \psi(x)
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
	\psi(x) = \bra{x}\ket{\psi} &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}} \int \mathrm dp \bra{x}\ket{p} \bra{p}\ket{\psi} \\
				    &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}} \int\mathrm dp \ e^{ixp/\hbar} \tilde \psi(p). \\
\end{align*}
Now given $ H(\hat x, \hat p) $ we can consider the time independent Schr\"odinger equation
\[
  H\ket \psi = E \ket \psi.
\]
If $ H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V(\hat x) $ then on $ \psi(x) $ we get that
\[
	H\to -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)
\]
and on $ \tilde \psi(p) $ we get that
\[
	H\to \frac{p^2}{2m} + V\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\right).
\]
It may  be easy to interpret the potential term in momentum space for something like $ V(x) = \lambda x^n $ which gives
\[
	V\left(i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial p}\right) = \lambda(i\hbar)^n\left(\frac\partial{\partial p}\right)^n
\]
for a positive integer $ n $. More generally this isn't obvious what the potential term in momentum space means. We will argue from first principles as follows.\par
Consider
\begin{align*}
	\bra{p} V(\hat x) \ket{\psi} &= \int\mathrm dx\bra p V(\hat x) \ket x \bra{x}\ket{\psi} \\
				     &= \int\mathrm dx \int\mathrm dp'\bra p V(\hat x) \ket x \bra{x}\ket{p'} \bra{p'}\ket{\psi} \\
				     &= \int\mathrm dp'\left(\int\mathrm dx \ V(x)\bra{p}\ket{x} \bra{x}\ket{p'}\bra{p'}\ket{\psi}\right) \\
				     &= \int\mathrm dp'\int\mathrm dx \ V(x) \frac{e^{-i(p-p')x/\hbar}}{2\pi \hbar} \tilde \psi(p) \\
				     &= \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{1/2}}\int\mathrm dp'\ \tilde V(p-p')\tilde \psi(p')
\end{align*}
Which is a reproduction of the convolution theorem. So $ H\ket \psi = E \ket \psi $ becomes
\[
	-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \psi''(x) + V(x)\psi(x) =E\psi(x)
\]
in position form. Or
\[
	\frac{p^2}{2m}\tilde \psi(p) + \frac 1{(2\pi\hbar)^{1/2}} \int\mathrm dp'\tilde V(p-p') \tilde \psi(p') = E\tilde \psi(p)
\]
in momentum space.\par
To generalise from one to three dimensions we use the commutation relation that
\[
	[\hat x_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar \delta_{ij}.
\]
One complete commuting set is
\[
	\hat{\mathbf x} = (\hat x_1, \hat x_2, \hat x_3)
\]
with joint eigenstates
\[
	\hat x_i\ket{\mathbf x} = x_i\ket{\mathbf x}
\]
which are orthonormal, so $ \bra{\mathbf x}\ket{\mathbf x'} = \delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x- \mathbf x') $. \par
An alternative set
\[
	\hat{\mathbf p} = (\hat p_1, \hat p_2,\hat p_3)
\]
with joint eigenstates
\[
	\hat p_i \ket{\mathbf p} = p_i\ket{\mathbf p}
\]
which are orthonormal in the same sense as the position operator. For any state $ \ket \psi $, we can expand
\begin{align*}
	\ket \psi = \int \mathrm d^3 \mathbf x\ \psi(\mathbf x)\ket{\mathbf x} = \int\mathrm d^3 \mathbf p\ \tilde \psi(\mathbf p)\ket{\mathbf p}
\end{align*}
where $ \psi(\mathbf x)=\bra{\x}\ket{\psi} $ and $ \tilde \psi(\mathbf p) = \bra{\mathbf p} \ket{\psi}$. We also get that
\[
\bra{\x}\ket{\mathbf p} = \frac 1{(2\pi\hbar)^{3/2}} e^{i\mathbf p \cdot\mathbf x/\hbar}
\]
and the wavefunctions are related by
\[
	\tilde \psi(\mathbf p) = \frac 1{(2\pi \hbar)^{3/2}} \int \mathrm d^3 \mathbf x \ e^{i\mathbf p \cdot\mathbf x/\hbar} \psi(\mathbf x)
\]
and the corresponding Fourier inverse.
\subsubsection{Position and Momentum Eigenstates}
Now we return to one dimension and seek to justify the result for $ \bra{x}\ket{p} $ using only the commutator relation. Given $ \ket x $ with $ \hat x \ket x = x\ket x $, consider
\[
	T(a) = e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac 1{n!} \left(\frac{-ia}{\hbar}\right)^n \hat p^n
\]
and note that
\[
[\hat x,T(a)] = \left[\hat x, \frac{-ia\hat p}{\hbar}\right]T(a) = aT(a).
\]
Hence 
\begin{align*}
	\hat x(T(x)\ket x) &= ([\hat x, T(a)]+T(a)\hat x)\ket x \\
			   &= (x+a)(T(x)\ket x)
\end{align*}
so we can think of $ T(a)\ket x $ as a translated eigenstate.\par
Similarly consider $ S(b) = e^{ib\hat x/\hbar} $ and note that $ [\hat p, S(b)] = bS(b) $. Hence
\[
  \hat p\ket p = p \ket p \implies \hat p (S(b)\ket p) = (p+b)(S(b)\ket p)
\]
so $ S(b) $ gives a translation in momentum space.
\par
Now take fixed values $ x_0 $ and $ p_0 $ and define all eigenstates by
\[
	\ket{x_0+a} = T(a) \ket{x_0},\quad \ket{p_0+b} = S(b) \ket{p_0}.
\]
Now observe that 
\begin{align*}
	\bra{x_0+a}\ket{p_0+b} &= \bra{x_0} e^{ia\hat p/\hbar}\ket{p_0+b} \\
			       &= \bra{x_0} e^{ia(p_0+b)/\hbar}e^{ib\hat x/\hbar} \ket {p_0} \\
			       &= e^{(ab +ap_0 + bx_0)/\hbar}\bra{x_0}\ket{p_0}. \\
\end{align*}
Now choose $ \bra{x_0}\ket{p_0} = \frac 1{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}} $ for correct and consistent normalisation. Then set $ x_0 = p_0 = 0 $, so we get
\[
	\bra {a}\ket {b} = \frac 1{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} e^{iab/\hbar}.
\]
\section{Evolution in Time and Pictures}
\subsection{The Schr\"odinger Equation and the Schr\"odinger Picture}
The evolution in time of state $ \ket {\psi(t)} $ is governed (in absence of external influences of measurement) by the Sch\"odinger equation
\[
	i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ket {\psi(t)} = H\ket{\psi(t)}
\]
where $ H = H^\dagger $ is the Hamiltonian.\par
Equivalently
\[
	-i\hbar \frac\partial{\partial t}\bra{\psi(t)} = \bra{\psi(t)} H.
\]
This implies that
\[
	\frac \partial{\partial t}\left(\bra{\psi(t)}\ket{\psi(t)}\right) = 0
\]
so that the normalisation of $ \ket{\psi(t)} $ is preserved in time.\par
The solution of the Schr\"odinger equation can be written as
\[
	\ket{\psi(t)} = U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}
\]
where $ U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar} $ is the time-evolution operator, assuming that $ H $ is independent of $ t $. We can check immediately from $ i\hbar \frac{\partial U(t)}{\partial t} = HU(t) $.\par
Recall that time-evolution problems can be conveniently solved by working with basis of energy eigenstates $ \{\ket n\} $ with
\[
  H\ket n = E_n\ket n.
\]
with
\[
	e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\ket n
\]
being a solution of the time-dependent Schr\"odinger equation.
\par
Generally if
\[
	\ket{\psi(0)} = \sum_n\alpha_n \ket{n}
\]
where 
\[
  H\ket n = E_n\ket n
\]
then
\[
	\ket {\psi(t)} = \sum_n\alpha_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar} \ket n.
\]
\begin{example}
  Consider a system with two energy eigenstates $ \ket 1 $ and $ \ket 2 $. Consider measuring $ Q $ defined by
  \[
    Q\ket 1 = \ket 2, \quad Q\ket 2 = \ket 1
  \]
  So $ [Q,H]\ne 0 $. The eigenstates of $ Q $ are
  \[
	  \ket \pm = \frac 1{\sqrt 2}(\ket 1 \pm \ket 2)
  \]
  with eigenvalues $ q_\pm = \pm 1 $. Take the initial state
  \[
	  \ket {\psi(0)} = \ket +.
  \]
  This implies that
  \[
	  \ket{\psi(t)} = \frac 1{\sqrt 2}\left(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\ket 1 + e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\ket 2\right)
  \]
  so the probability of measuring $ Q $ at time $ t $ and getting $ \pm 1 $ is
  \begin{align*}
	  |\bra{\pm}\ket{\psi(t)}|^2 &= \left|\frac 12 (\bra{1}\pm\ket{2})(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\ket{1}+e^{-iE_2t/\hbar} \ket 2)\right|^2 \\
				     &= \left|\frac 12(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\pm e^{-iE_2t/\hbar})\right|^2 \\
				     &= \begin{cases}
					     \cos^2(E_1-E_2)t/2\hbar \\
					     \sin^2(E_1-E_2)t/2\hbar
				     \end{cases}. \\
  \end{align*}
  In the basis 
  \begin{align*}
	  \ket  1 &\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
      1 \\ 0
    \end{pmatrix} \\
		  \ket 2 &\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
		    0 \\ 1
		  \end{pmatrix}
  \end{align*}
  then
  \[
    H\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
       E_1 & 0 \\
       0 & E_2
    \end{pmatrix}
  \]
  and
  \[
	  U= e^{-iHt/\hbar} \longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
	     e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} & 0 \\
	     0 & e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}.
	  \end{pmatrix}
  \]
  But instead we can work in the alternate basis where
  \begin{align*}
	  \ket + &\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
	    1 \\ 0
	  \end{pmatrix} \\
		  \ket - &\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
		    0 \\ 1
		  \end{pmatrix}
  \end{align*}
  So we have that
  \begin{align*}
	  H \ket \pm &= \frac 1{\sqrt 2}(E_1\ket 1 + E_2\ket 2)\\
	  \bra \pm H\ket \pm &= \frac 12(E_1+E_2) = \ep \\
	  \bra \mp H \ket \mp &= \frac 12(E_1-E_2) = \hbar\lambda
  \end{align*}
  where we define $ \ep $ and $ \lambda $ to be constants. Then we have that
  \[
    H\longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
	    \ep & \hbar \lambda \\
	    \hbar\lambda & \ep
    \end{pmatrix} = \ep I + \hbar\lambda M
  \]
  where $ I $ is the identity matrix and $ M $ is the matrix $ \begin{pmatrix}
     0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0
  \end{pmatrix} $ so now we have that
  \begin{align*}
	  U(t) &= e^{-iHt/\hbar} = e^{-it(\ep I + \lambda\hbar M)/\hbar} \\
	       &= e^{-i t \ep/\hbar}e^{-it\lambda M}.
  \end{align*}
  Note that $ M^2 = I $ and hence we have that
  \begin{align*}
	  \mathrm{exp}(-it\lambda M)& = \sum_{r=0}^\infty \frac 1{(2r)!} (-i\lambda r)^{2r} I + \sum_{r=0}^\infty \frac 1{(2r+1)!}(-i\lambda t)^{2r+1} M \\
				    &= (\cos\lambda t) I -i (\sin\lambda t) M.
  \end{align*}
  So we have that 
  \begin{align*}
	  \ket{\psi(0)} = \ket + \longrightarrow \begin{pmatrix}
	    1 \\ 0
	  \end{pmatrix}
  \end{align*}
  so
  \[
	  \ket{\psi(t)} = e^{-iHt/\hbar} \ket +\longrightarrow e^{-it\ep/\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}
	    \cos\lambda t \\
	    -i\sin\lambda t
	  \end{pmatrix}
  \]
  which is therefore 
  \[
	  e^{-it\ep/\hbar}(\cos\lambda t \ket + - i\sin\lambda t\ket -).
  \]
  Now compare with previous result for probability of measuring $ W $ and finding $ \pm 1 $.
\end{example}
\subsection{Heisenberg Picture and Heisenberg Equations}
\subsubsection{Unitary operators}
Physical predictions in quantum mechanics are given by probability amplitudes, eigenvalues, expectation values, etc, hence we have expressions like
\[
	\bra{\psi}\ket{\phi},\quad \bra{\phi}A\ket{\psi}
\]
and similar things. 
\begin{definition}
	(Unitary) An operator $ U $ is called \textit{unitary} if
	\[
	  UU^\dagger = U^\dagger U = I
	\]
	or equivalently
	\[
		U^\dagger = \inv U.
	\]
\end{definition}
Given any such $ U $ consider a map on states and operators taking
\begin{align*}
	\ket \psi &\longrightarrow U\ket \psi = \ket{\psi'} \\
	\bra \psi &\longrightarrow \bra \psi U^\dagger = \bra{\psi'} \\
	A&\longrightarrow  UAU^\dagger = A'.
\end{align*}
Under this, all physical predictions remain unchanged sinc we have that
\[
	\bra{\phi}\ket{\psi} \longrightarrow \bra{\phi'}\ket{\psi'} = \bra{\phi} U^\dagger U\ket{\psi} = \bra{\phi}\ket{\psi}
\]
and
\[
	\bra{\phi}A\ket{\psi} \longrightarrow \bra{\phi'}A'\ket{\psi'} = \bra{\phi}U^\dagger UAU^\dagger U \ket{\psi} = \bra{\phi}A\ket{\psi}.
\]
Furthermore we have that
\[
  C = AB\longrightarrow C'=A'B'
\]
and
\[
	C =[A,B] \longrightarrow C'=[A',B'].
\]
We also have that if $ Q $ is Hermitian, then $ Q' $ is also Hermitian. We also have the implication that 
\begin{align*}
	Q\ket{\psi} = \lambda \ket{\psi} \implies Q'\ket{\psi'} = \lambda \ket{\psi'}
\end{align*}
with the same eigenvalue $ \lambda $.
\subsubsection{Changing Pictures}
In the Schr\"odinger picture we have that
\begin{align*}
	i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \ket{\psi(t)} &= H \ket{\psi(t)}\\ 
	\implies \ket{\psi(t)} &= U(t) \ket{\psi(0)}
\end{align*}
where $ U(t) = \mathrm{exp}(-iHt/\hbar) $ is unitary. In fact we have that $ U(t)^\dagger = U(-t) = \inv{U(t)} $ and that $ U(t_1)U(t_2) = U(t_1 + t_2) $. The states depend on $ t $ but observables do not in the Schr\"odinger picture. To pass to the Heisenberg picture we shift $ t $-dependence from states to operators as follows. In the Schr\"odinger picture we have states and operators
\[
	\ket{\psi(t)}_{\mathrm S} \quad A_{\mathrm S}.
\]
But in the Heisenberg picture we have 
\begin{align*}
	\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm H} &= U^\dagger \ket{\psi(t)}_\mathrm{S} = e^{itH/\hbar} \ket{\psi(t)}_{\mathrm{S}} = \ket{\psi(0)}_{\mathrm S} \\
	A_{\mathrm H}(t) &= U^\dagger A_{\mathrm S} U = e^{itH/\hbar}A_{\mathrm S} e^{-itH/\hbar}
\end{align*}
and because the transformation $ U(t) $ is unitary we have that
\[
	\bra{\phi(t)} A_{\mathrm S} \ket{\psi(t)}_{\mathrm S} = \bra{\phi}A_{\mathrm H}(t)\ket{\psi}_{\mathrm H}
\]
Note that $ H_{\mathrm H} = H_{\mathrm S} = H $.\par
To specify dynamics in the Heisenberg picture consider
\begin{align*}
	\frac d{dt} A_{\mathrm H} (t) &= \frac d{dt} \left(e^{iHt/\hbar} A_{\mathrm S} e^{-iHt/\hbar}\right) \\
				      &= \frac{iH}\hbar e^{iHt/\hbar} A_{\mathrm S} e^{-iHt/\hbar} - e^{iHt/\hbar}A_{\mathrm S} e^{-iHt/\hbar} \frac{iH}\hbar \\
				      &= \frac i\hbar [H,A_{\mathrm H}(t)] 
\end{align*}
or
\[
	i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}A_{\mathrm H} (t) = [A_{\mathrm H}(t), H]
\]
which is the Heisenberg equations of motion.
\begin{example}
	Consider a particle in one dimension $ \hat x(t), \hat p(t) $ in the Heisenberg picture. Consider $ H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V(\hat x) $ so the Heisenberg equations of motion become
	\[
		\frac {d}{dt} \hat x(t) = \frac 1{i\hbar} [\hat x(t), H] = \frac 1 m \hat p(t)
	\]
	and
	\[
		\frac {d}{dt} \hat p(t) = \frac 1{i\hbar} [\hat p(t), H] = -V' (\hat x(t))
	\]
	using $ [\hat x(t),\hat p(t)] = i\hbar $.
\end{example}
Taking expectation values in any state $ \ket \psi $ (now independent of $ t $ in the Heisenberg picture) gives
\[
	\frac d{dt} \langle \hat x\rangle = \frac 1m \langle \hat p \rangle
\]
and
\[
	\frac d{dt} \langle \hat p\rangle = - \langle V'(\hat x)\rangle
\]
which is Ehrenfest's theorem, which is true in all pictures. For some potentials we can solve the Heisenberg question. For example if $ V=0 $ we have a free particle, so
\[
	\frac d{dt} \hat p(t) = 0
\]
which means that $ \hat p(t) = \hat p(0) $ which is a constant operator. And therefore $ \hat x(t) = \hat x(0) + \frac{\hat p(0)}m t $ like classical dynamics. If we have $ V = \frac 12 m\omega^2 x^2 $ for a oscillator then we get the set of equations
\[
  \begin{cases}
	  \frac d{dt} \hat x(t) = \frac 1m \hat p(t) \\
	  \frac d{dt} \hat p(t) = -m\omega^2 \hat x(t)
  \end{cases}
\]
which gives that
\begin{align*}
	\frac{d^2}{dt^2} \hat x + \omega^2 \hat x &= 0\\
	\frac{d^2}{dt^2} \hat p + \omega^2 \hat p &= 0
\end{align*}
which as a solution gives
\[
  \begin{cases}
	  \hat x(t) = \hat x(0) \cos\omega t + \frac{\hat p(0)}m \sin\omega t \\
	  \hat p(t) = \hat p(0) \cos\omega t - m\omega^2 \hat x(0) \sin\omega t
  \end{cases}
\]
or equivalently
\[
  \begin{cases}
	  \hat x(t) = \left(\frac\hbar{2m\omega}\right)^{1/2}(ae^{-i\omega t} + a^\dagger e^{i\omega t}) \\
	  \hat p(t) = \left(\frac{\hbar m\omega}2\right)^{1/2}\frac 1i (ae^{-i\omega t} - a^\dagger e^{i\omega t})
  \end{cases}
\]
\subsection{Canonical Quantisation (Non-examinable)}
This is the final step in Dirac's approach to constructing quantum mechanics. How do we pass from a general classical system to its quantum version? Any classical system can be described by a set of generalised positions $ x_i(t) $ and momenta $ p_i(t) $ with $ 1\le i \le N $ which may include angles and angular momenta. These are coordinates in \textit{phase space}. Dynamics are governed by some Hamiltonian $ H(x_i,p_i) $. The \textit{Poisson bracket} of any functions $ f(x_i,p_i), g(x_i,p_i) $ is defined by
\[
	\{ f,g\} = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}
\]
which is a new function on phase space. The properties of Poisson brackets closely resemble those of commutators. Dynamics in this formulation given by Hamilton's equation 
\[
	\frac {df}{dt} = \{ f, H\}.
\]
In \textit{cononical quantisation} define quantum theory by taking classical funtions to quantum operators and Poisson brackets determine commutators.
\[
	[\hat f,\hat g] = i\hbar \hat{\{f,g\}}
\]
which recovers $ [\hat x_i,\hat p_j] = i\hbar \delta_{ij} $ and Heisenberg's equation from Hamilton's equation.
\section{Composite Systems and Identical Particles}
\subsection{Tensor Products}
This is a general way of constructing quantum systems from simplier subsystems. Suppose that 
\begin{align*}
  \ket \psi \in V_1,\quad \ket \phi \in V_2
\end{align*}
are states in spaces for two systems. The tensor product space
\[
  V = V_1 \otimes V_2
\]
which consists of all linear combinations of tensor product states
\[
  \ket \psi \otimes \ket \phi
\]
subject to
\begin{enumerate}
	\item $ (\ket \psi + \ket {\psi'}) \otimes \ket \phi = \ket \psi \oplus \ket \phi + \ket {\psi'} \otimes \ket \phi $;
	\item $ \ket \phi \otimes (\ket \phi + \ket{\phi'}) = \ket \psi \otimes \ket \phi + \ket \psi \otimes \ket{\phi'} $;
		\item $ (\alpha \ket \psi) \otimes \ket \phi = \ket\psi\otimes (\alpha\ket \phi) = \alpha(\ket \psi\otimes \ket \phi) $.
\end{enumerate}
and similarly for duals.\par
The inner product on $ V $ satisfies
\[
	(\bra{\psi'}\otimes \bra {\phi'})(\ket \psi \otimes \ket\phi) = \bra{\psi'}\ket{\psi}\bra{\phi'}\ket{\phi}
\]

and extend by linearity. Given operators $ A $ and $ B $ on $ V_1 $ and $ V_2 $ respectively, we define an operator $ A\otimes B $ on $ V $ by
\[
	(A\otimes B)(\ket \psi \otimes \ket\phi) = (A\ket \psi)\otimes (B\ket\phi)
\]
and again extend by linearity. In particular, $ A\longrightarrow A\otimes I $ and $ B\longrightarrow I \otimes B $. Operators of this form commute for any $ A,B $. Consider a particle in two dimensions with position operators $ \hat x_1, \hat x_2 $, The basis of join eigensates constructed as $ \ket{x_1,x_2} $ we can take $ \ket{x_1,x_2}=\ket{x_1}\otimes \ket{x_2} $. This makes it obvious that $ [\hat x_i,\hat x_j] = 0 $ for $ i\ne j $.\par
The wavefunction for $ \ket \psi \otimes \ket \phi $ is now given by
\begin{align*}
	\bra{x_1,x_2}(\ket \psi \otimes \ket\phi) &= (\bra {x_1}\otimes \bra{x_2})(\ket\psi\otimes \ket \phi) \\
						  &= \bra{x_1}\ket {\psi}\bra{x_2}\ket{\phi} \\
						  &= \psi(x_1)\phi(x_2).
\end{align*}
This gives a correspondence between tensor product state and separable wavefunctions. Hence there is a correspondence between the tensor product space and all linaer combinations of such wavefunctions.
\begin{remark}
	If we have basis $ \{\ket n\} $ for $ V_1 $ and $ \{\ket m\} $ for $ V_2 $ then $ \{\ket n\otimes \ket {m}\} $ is a basis for $ V $. If basis are finite then the dimension of the tensor produce space is the product of the dimensions.
\end{remark}

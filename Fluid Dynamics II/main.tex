\section{Introduction}
The Part IB Fluid Dynamics course mainly covers inviscid flow and irrotational flow including potential flow. The course covered an introduction to viscous flow for the very simple case where $ \mathbf u = (u(y,t), 0) $ in 2D parallel flow.
\par
This course is about the effects of viscosity and answers the following very important questions
\begin{enumerate}
	\item How does viscosity affect the equations for a general flow?
	\item What happens if viscosity dominates over the inertia of the fluid?
	\item What happens if the flow is nearly parallel?
	\item What happens if viscosity is small compared to inertia?
	\item What about stability?
\end{enumerate}
\section{Equations for a Newtonian Viscous Flow}
\subsection{Dynamic viscosity}
Recall from Part IB Fluid Dynamics that we have Couette flow, in which a plate at rest and a plate moving with velocity $ U $ are parallel, with a gap $ h $ between them filled with a viscous fluid. For a simple fluid, we find that the force required to move the top plate $ F $ is proportional to $ \frac {UA}h $. Hence the shear stress is
\[
  \tau_s = \frac FA = \mu\frac Uh
\]
where $ \mu $ is the dynamic viscosity. The shear stress is due to the relative motion between each layer of fluid and its neighbours above and below.
\subsection{Rate of strain tensor}
Consider the velocity near a fixed point $ \mathbf 0 $ \textit{without loss of generality}. Taylor expand $ \mathbf u $, so we have the multivariable Taylor expansion
\[
	u_i(\mathbf x) = u_i(0) + x_j\left.\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} \right|_{\mathbf 0} + \frac 12 x_jx_k\left.\frac{\partial^2 u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_k}\right|_{\mathbf 0} + \cdots
\]
We drop the quadratic terms and decompose $ \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} $ into symmetric and antisymmetric parts, so
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} &= \frac 12 \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) + \frac 12 \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) \\
					  &= e_{ij} + \frac 12 \varepsilon_{jik} \omega_k
\end{align*}
where $ e_{ij} $ is the \textit{rate of strain tensor} and $ \boldsymbol\omega = \nabla\times \mathbf u  $ is the vorticity. Hence we have that
\[
  \mathbf u (\mathbf x) \approx \mathbf u (\mathbf 0) + \mathbf e \cdot \mathbf x + \frac 12 \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf x.
\]
We have that $ e_{ij}= e_{ji} $ by definition so $ \mathbf e $ is real symmetric and hence has real orthogonal eigenvectors, which are the principal axes of strain. For an incompressible flow we have that $ e_{ii} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 $ so the eigenvalues sum to $ 0 $. The eigenvalues are called the principal rates of strain.
\subsection{The stress tensor}
Recall in mechanics we consider two types of forces:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Body forces $ \mathbf f $ that act per unit volume, such as gravity;
	\item Surface forces $ \boldsymbol \tau $ that act per unit area, for example, pressure and viscous shear stress
\end{enumerate}
Let $ \boldsymbol\tau (\mathbf n; \mathbf x, t) \delta S $ denote the force exerted by the positive side on the negative side of the small area element with $ \mathbf n $ pointing from the negative side to the positive side. \par
For example, we have that $ \boldsymbol\tau(\mathbf n) = - p\mathbf n $ for an inviscid fluid. \par
Now let $ \mathbf n \delta S $ be the sloping face of a small tetrahedron of size $ \ell $ whose three other faces are aligned with the coordinate planes. The force acting on the sloped face is $ \boldsymbol \tau(\mathbf n)\delta S $. The forces on the other faces are $ \boldsymbol\tau(-\mathbf 1)\delta S_1, \boldsymbol\tau(-\mathbf 2)\delta S_2, \boldsymbol\tau(-\mathbf 3)\delta S_3 $. Considering the force balance, the body force and inertia are $ O(\ell^3) $ and the surface forces $ \boldsymbol \tau $ are $ O(\ell^2) $, so their ratio is $ O(\ell)\to 0 $ as $ \ell \to 0 $. So the surface forces must balance themselves for small $ \ell $.\par
Note that pressure and viscous stresses balance inertia and gravity on the characteristic length scale, so small $ \ell $ means that $ \ell $ is small compared to the characteristic length scale $ L $.\par
Since the forces balance, we have that
\[
  \boldsymbol\tau(\mathbf n ) \delta S + \boldsymbol \tau(-\mathbf 1)\delta S_1 + \boldsymbol\tau (-\mathbf 2)\delta S_2 + \boldsymbol\tau(-\mathbf 3)\delta S_3 = 0.
\]
Now
\begin{align*}
  \delta S_i = \mathbf i \cdot (\mathbf n \delta S) = n_i \delta S
\end{align*}
and $ \boldsymbol \tau(-\mathbf 1) = - \boldsymbol \tau(\mathbf 1) $ by Newton's Third Law. Substituting these in, we get
\[
  \boldsymbol \tau(\mathbf n) = \boldsymbol \tau (\mathbf 1) n_1 + \boldsymbol \tau(\mathbf 2) n_2 + \boldsymbol \tau(\mathbf 3) n_3
\]
which shows that $ \boldsymbol \tau $ is linear in $ \mathbf n $. So we have that
\[
	\tau_i(\mathbf n) = \sigma_{ij} n_j
\]
where $ \sigma_{ij} = \tau_i(\mathbf j) $.
\begin{definition}
	(Stress tensor) We call $ \sigma(\mathbf x,t) $, as constructed above, the \textit{stress tensor}.
\end{definition}
The key result is that the stress $ \boldsymbol\tau $ exerted on a small area element (by the outside on the inside) is given by $ \boldsymbol\tau(\mathbf n) = \sigma\cdot \mathbf n $.\par
For the inviscid case $ \sigma = -p\,\mathbf I $, i.e. $ \sigma_{ij} = -p\delta_{ij} $, where $ \mathbf I $ is the identity tensor.
\par
We will now show that $ \sigma $ is a symmetric tensor.
\par
Consider the angular momentum balance on a small ($ \ell \ll L $) arbitrary volume $ V $ about a fixed point in $ V $, which we call $ \mathbf 0 $.
\par
The moment of the surface forces is
\begin{align*}
	\int_{\partial V} \varepsilon_{ijk} x_j \sigma_{km}n_m \mathrm dS
\end{align*}
which is $ O(\ell^3) $. Similarly, with $ \rho $ the density, the moment of the body forces and inertia is
\[
  \int_V \mathbf x \times \left(\mathbf f - \rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}\right) \mathrm dV
\]
which is also $ O(\ell^4) $.
\par
For small $ \ell $, the moments of the surface forces must balance, hence
\[
   0 = \int_V \varepsilon_{ijk}\frac{\partial }{\partial x_m} (x_j \sigma_{km} )\mathrm dV = \int_V \varepsilon_{ijk}\left(\sigma_{kj} + x_j \frac{\partial \sigma_{km}}{\partial x_m}\right)\mathrm dV.
\]
Since $ x_j $ is $ O(\ell) $, we can take $ \ell \to 0 $ to get rid of the second term, so the integral is approximately
\[
	\int_V \varepsilon_{ijk} \sigma_{kj} \mathrm dV.
\]
Since $ V $ is arbitrary, $ \varepsilon_{ijk} \sigma_{kj} = 0 $, hence $ \sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji} $.
\subsection{Constitutive Equation for a Newtonian Fluid}
We divide the stress tensor into two terms:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item An isotropic pressure term $ -p\delta_{ij} $, with $ p = -\frac 13 \sigma_{kk} $.
	\item A deviatoric stress $ \sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm{dev}} $ which is the deviation from isotropic and so is traceless: $ \sigma_{kk}^{\mathrm{dev}} = 0 $.
\end{enumerate}
So we have that
\[
	\sigma_{ij} = -p\delta_{ij} + \sigma_{ij}^\mathrm{dev}.
\]
The deviatoric stress is due to frictional interactions with neighbouring fluid particles sliding past with slightly different velocities. For fluid at rest, we must have a static equilibrium where $ \nabla p = \mathbf f $. Relative motion in the neighbourhood of a point is given by the velocity gradient, so we expect that
\[
	\sigma^{\mathrm{dev}} = \mathcal F[\nabla \mathbf u]
\]
and $ \mathcal F(\mathbf 0) = \mathbf 0 $. A Newtonian fluid is defined by two properties.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item $ \dev $ is linear in the local, instantaneous value of $ \nabla \mathbf u $, hence we have that
		\[
			\dev_{ij} = A_{ijk\ell} \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_\ell}
		\]
		for some tensor $ A_{ijk\ell} $.\par
		Local instananeous is good for simple (Newtonian) fluids for which $ \ell_{\mathrm{mol}} \sim 10^{-9} \ \mathrm m$ and $ t_{\mathrm{mol}} \sim 10^{-12} \ \mathrm s $. This can go wrong since we can have action at a distance such as a flow which contains a suspension of fibres. The fluid might also have memory due to deformation elasticity such as a solution contain long-chain polymers.
	\item The fluid is locally isotropic. This is not true for example in a liquid crystal. So $ A_{ijk\ell} $ must be an isotropic tensor, hence
		\[
			A_{ijk\ell} = \mu' \delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell} + \mu'' \delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell} + \mu'''\delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk}.
		\]
\end{enumerate}
Together this gives us that
\begin{align*}
	\dev_{ij} = \mu'\delta_{ij}\frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_k} + \mu'' \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} + \mu''' \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
\end{align*}
and luckily for us the first term is zero since the flow is assumed to be incompressible. Now since $ \sigma $ is symmetric we have that $ \mu'' = \mu''' $, so let's drop the primes and call them both $ \mu $.\par
This gives us 
\begin{align*}
	\sigma_{ij} & =-p \delta_{ij} + \mu \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) \\
	&= - p \delta_{ij} + 2\mu e_{ij}.
\end{align*}
This is called the constitutive relationship for an incompressible Newtonian viscous fluid.
\begin{remark}
  We'll make some remarks on this relationship.
  \begin{enumerate}
	  \item No dependence on the vorticity $ \boldsymbol\omega = \nabla\times\mathbf u $, only depends on the strain-stress rate $ e = \frac 12 (\nabla \mathbf u + \nabla\mathbf u^\top) $, since localy solid-body rotation $ \frac 12 \boldsymbol\omega \times \x $ doesn't involve sliding or friction.
	  \item Many common fluids (air, water, oil, syrup) match our assumptions for getting to these equations up to a very close approximation. 
	  \item $ \mu $ here is the same as in IB FLuid Dynamics. Since if we take a flow $ \mathbf u = (u(y),0,0) $ we find that $ \sigma_{xy} = \mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} $ as we did last year.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}
\subsection{The Momentum Equation}
\subsubsection{The Cauchy Momentum Equation}
The momentum in an arbitrary fixed volume $ V $ can change with time due to 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Momentum being carried across the boundary $ \partial V $;
	\item Applying body forces acting on $ V $;
	\item Applying surface forces on $ \partial V $.
\end{enumerate}
So we have that
\begin{align*}
	\frac d{dt} \int_V \rho u_i \mathbf dV = -\int_{\partial V} \rho u_i(\mathbf u \cdot\mathbf n)\mathrm dS + \int_V f_i \mathrm dV + \int_{\partial V}\sigma_{ij}n_j\mathrm dS.
\end{align*}
We'll use that fact that $ V $ is fixed and apply the divergence theorem, so
\begin{align*}
	\int_V \frac\partial{\partial t} (\rho u_i)\mathrm dV + \int_V\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(\rho u_iu_j)\mathrm dV =  \int_V \left(f_i+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\sigma_{ij}\right)\mathrm dV.
\end{align*}
Now since $ V $ is arbitrary we can drop the integral, so we get
\[
u_i\left(\frac {\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(\rho u_j)\right) + \rho \left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) = f_i + \frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j}.
\]
Notice that the first bracketed term is the left-hand side of the mass conservation equation, so it is zero. This gives us the Cauchy momentum equation,
\[
	\rho \frac{D\mathbf u }{Dt} = \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u \cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = \mathbf f + \nabla\cdot\sigma.
\]
\subsubsection{The Navier-Stokes Equation}
For a Newtonian fluid $ \sigma_{ij} = - p\delta_{ij} + 2\mu e_{ij} $. We assume that $ \mu $ is uniform in the fluid, so we get that 
\[
	\frac \partial{\partial x_j}\sigma_{ij} = - \frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j}\delta_{ij} + \mu\left(\frac{\partial^2 u_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial^2 u_j}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}\right).
\]
And we can use the fact that the fluid is incompressible, to get the \textit{Navier-Stokes equation}
\[
	\rho \frac{D \mathbf u}{Dt} = \mathbf f - \nabla p + \mu\nabla^2 \mathbf u.
\]
\subsubsection{Boundary conditions}
 For a material boundary with normal $ \mathbf n $ we have that
 \begin{enumerate}
	 \item Mass conservations gives that $ \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf n $ is continuous;
	 \item $ \nabla \mathbf u $ is finite because we need $ \sigma $ to be finite, so $ \mathbf u - (\mathbf u \cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n $ is continuous for a  viscous fluid. This is the no slip condition. These together mean that $ \mathbf u $ is continuous.
	 \item From Newton's Third law we get that $ \sigma \cdot \mathbf n $ is continuous since the stresses must balance on the boundary. i.e the body stress on the fluid is the negative of the fluid stress on the solid.
 \end{enumerate}
 \subsection{Energy Equation and Viscous Dissipation}
Obtain a mechanical energy equation by dotting the Cauchy momentum equation with $ \mathbf u $ so we get
\begin{align*}
	u_i \rho\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\right) &= u_if_i + u_i\frac{\partial \sigma_{ij}}{\partial x_j} \\
	\frac \partial{\partial t} \left(\frac 12 \rho u^2\right) - \frac 12 u^2 \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}+ \frac\partial{\partial x_j} \left(\frac 12 \rho u^2  u_j\right) - \frac 12 u^2 \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(\rho u_j) &= u_if_i +\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(u_i\sigma_{ij}) - \sigma_{ij}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}.
\end{align*}
Now we integrate over a fixed volume $ V $ to get the following equation.
\begin{definition}
	(Energy Equation)
	\[
		\frac d{dt} \int_V \frac 12 \rho u^2\  \mathrm dV + \int_{\partial V} \frac 12 \rho u^2(\mathbf u \cdot\mathbf n) \ \mathrm dS = \int_V \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf f\  \mathrm dV + \int_{\partial V}\mathbf u \cdot \sigma \mathbf n \ \mathrm dS - \int_V \sigma_{ij}e_{ij}\ \mathrm dV.
	\]
\end{definition}
Now we can explain each term in the equation in the order as written.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The rate of change of kinetic energy inside $ V $.
	\item Kinetic energy being carried across the boundary $ \partial V $.
	\item The rate of doing work by body forces inside $ V $.
	\item The rate of doing work by surface forces $ \boldsymbol \tau = \sigma \cdot\mathbf n $ on $ \partial V $.
	\item The rate of doing work against the internal stresses due to deformation.
\end{enumerate}
For incompressible Newtonian fluids $ \sigma_{ij} = -p \delta_{ij} + 2\mu e_{ij} $ so we have that
\[
	\sigma_{ij}e_{ij} = -p e_{ii} + 2\mu e_{ij}e_{ij}
\]
and $ e_{ii} $ vanishes since the flow is incompressible.\par
Hence in the case for an incompressible fluid, the fifth term gives the rate of loss of mechanical energy (dissipation) due to viscosity and defomration. The energy lost if converted to heat and is a source term in the temperature equation.
\begin{definition}
	(Localy viscous dissipation rate) We define $ \Phi $ as the \textit{viscous dissipation rate} given as
	\[
		\Phi = 2\mu e_{ij}e_{ij}.
	\]
\end{definition}
\subsection{Nondimensionalisation}
Suppose a flow has a characteristic length scale $ L $ and velocity scale $ U $ and varies on a timescale $ T $. Assume no body forces so we get that
\[
	\rho\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = - \nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf u, \quad \nabla \cdot\mathbf u = 0
\]
where $ \mathbf u = \mathbf u(\x,t,\rho,\mu,U,L,T,\text{shape},\dots) $. Define a dimensionless velocity $ \mathbf u^* = \frac{\mathbf u}U $, position $ \x^* = \frac \x L $, time $ t^* = \frac tT $, pressure $ p^* = \frac p{\mu U/L} $ (an alternative inertial scaling sets $ p^{**} = \frac{p}{\rho U^2}) $.\par
And substitute these into the Navier-Stokes equation. This gives that
\begin{align*}
	\rho \left(\frac UT \frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t} + \frac {U^2}L \mathbf u^* \cdot \nabla^* \mathbf u^*\right) &= - \frac{\mu U}{L^2} \nabla^* p^* + \frac{\mu U}{L^2} {\nabla^*}^2 \mathbf u^*.
\end{align*}
So collecting constants, we get that
\[
	\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathrm{St}\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t^*} + (\mathbf u^* \cdot\nabla)\mathbf u^*\right) = -\nabla^* p^* + {\nabla^*}^2 \mathbf u^*
\]
where we have the following defintions
\begin{definition}
	(Reynolds number) For a flow with length scale $ L $, velocity scale $ U $, density $ \rho $, and dynamic velocity $ \mu $ we have
	\[
		\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho UL}{\mu} = \frac{UL}{\nu}
	\]
	where we are defining 
	\[
		\nu = \frac{\mu}\rho
	\]
	to be the \textit{kinematic viscosity}.
\end{definition}
We also have $ \mathrm{St} = \frac L{UT} $ as the \textit{Stroughal number} although this comes up much less.
\par These are dimensionless quantities which we can use to characterise properties of the flow. \par
Now we can have $ \mathbf u^* = \mathbf u^*(\x^*,t,\mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{St}, \mathrm{Shape}) $ which is a function of fewer parameters. If the flow is steady then we can set $ T = \frac LU $, called the \textit{advection time}, and $ \mathrm{St} = 1 $, so the Reynolds number is the only important parameter. If the boundary conditions are oscillatory, then set $ T = \frac 1 \omega $, and then group 
\[
	\mathrm{Re}_T = \mathrm{Re} \cdot \mathrm{St}
\]
is called the \textit{unsteady Reynolds number} or the \textit{Stokes number}.
\[
	\mathrm{Re}_T = \frac{L^2}{\nu T} = \frac{\omega L^2}{\nu}
\]
which is a ratio of lengthscales.
\par
If two flows with different $ U,L,T $, have the same geometry and same $ \mathrm{Re}, \mathrm{St}, f $ and dimensionless boundary conditions, then they are \textit{dynamically similar} because they give the same dimensionless $ \mathbf u^* $.\par
In the limit with $ \mathrm{St} \ll 1 $ we have slow oscillations and hence we can neglect the $ \frac{\partial u^*}{\partial t^*} $ term to leading order which results in quasi-steady behaviour. Otherwise if $ \mathrm{St} \gg 1 $, we have rapid oscillations and we can neglect $ \mathbf u^*\cdot\nabla \mathbf u^* $ which results in a linearised set of equations. 
\par
If instead we have $ \mathrm{Re} \ll 1 $ the flow is viscously dominated and we neglect the LHS to get Stokes equations. If instead we have that $ \mathrm{Re}\gg 1 $ the flow is inertially dominated on length scale $ L $ which means we can often neglect $ {\nabla^*}^2 \mathbf u^* $ at most positions which results in the Euler equations, but not near rigid boundaries on lengthscales $ \ll 1 $, which gives our boundary layer equations.
\section{Unidirectional Flows}
Now start with the Navier-Stokes equations and have $ \sigma $ and dissipation $ \Phi $. Suppose that $ \mathbf u = (u,0,0) $. Incompressibility $ \nabla\cdot\mathbf u= 0 $ which implies that $ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0 $ which gives that $ (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla) \mathbf u =0 $ and hence $ \frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt} = \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t},0,0\right) $. Naiver-Stokes equation becomes
\begin{align*}
	\rho \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= f_x - \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} + \mu \nabla^2_{\perp} u \\
	\mathbf f_\perp &= \nabla_\perp p
\end{align*}
where $ u = u(y,z,t) $ and $ \perp $ indicates the components perpendicular to the flow direction. If $ \mathbf f =\mathbf 0 $ then 
\[
	\frac{\partial p}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = 0
\]
and 
\[
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0 \implies - \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} = G(t)
\]
independent of $ x $.
\subsection{Couette Flow}
Consider steady flow, driven by motion of the boundaries between two plates, the bottom of which is at rest, and the top plate which moves with velocity $ V $ in the positive $ x $ direction. Now from the previous equations we have that
\[
	0 =\mu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}
\]
along with boundary conditions $ u(0) = 0, u(h)=V $ which gives that $ =\frac Vh y $. Now
\[
	\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} = \begin{pmatrix}
		0 & \gamma & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & 0
	\end{pmatrix} \implies e_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
		0 & \frac{\gamma} 2 & 0 \\
		\frac{\gamma}2 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & 0
	\end{pmatrix}
	\implies \sigma_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix}
		-p & \mu\gamma & 0 \\
		\mu\gamma & -p & 0 \\
		0 & 0 & -p
	\end{pmatrix}
\]
e.g. the stress exerted by the bottom plate on the fluid is given by $ \sigma \cdot \mathbf n $ with $ \mathbf n = \begin{pmatrix}
	0 \\ -1 \\ 0
\end{pmatrix} $. This is equal to $ \begin{pmatrix}
  -\mu\gamma \\ p \\ 0
\end{pmatrix} $.\par
We have that $ \Phi = 2 \mu e_{ij}e_{ij} = 2\mu \left(\left(\frac \gamma 2\right)^2 + \left(\frac \gamma 2 \right)^2 \right) = \mu\gamma^2 $.
\subsection{Plane Poiseuille Flow}
Consider two plates both at rest placed at $ y=-h $ and $ y=h $. Suppose the pressure on the right is $ p_- $ and $ p_+ $ on the left. The result is steady flow driven by the pressure gradient $ \frac{dp}{dx} < 0 $. So now we have that
\[
	\mu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = \frac{dp}{dx}, \quad u(\pm h) = 0.
\]
This gives that $ u = -\frac 1{2\mu} \frac{dp}{dx}(h^2-y^2) $ and hence the flux per unit length 
\begin{align*}
	q = \int_{-h}^h u\ \mathrm dy = -\frac{2h^3}{3\mu} \frac{dp}{dx}.
\end{align*}
The vorticity $ \boldsymbol\omega = \nabla \times \mathbf u = \left(0,0,-\frac 1\mu \frac{dp}{dx} y\right) $ and the strain rate is $
  \begin{pmatrix}
     0 & \frac 12 \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} & 0 \\
     \frac 12 \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} & 0 & 0 \\
     0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix} $ with dissipation $ \Phi(y) = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)^2 $.
\begin{remark}
	Check the stress exerted \textit{by} the boundary \textit{on} the fluid at $ y = \pm h $ is $ \left(h \frac{dp}{dx}, \mp p, 0\right) $ and check the total dissipation $ \int_V \boldsymbol \Phi(y)\ \mathrm dV $ is equal to the rate of working by the stresses on $ \partial V $ which is $ \int_{\partial V} \mathbf u \cdot \sigma \cdot \mathbf n \ \mathrm dS $.
\end{remark}
\subsection{Circular Poiseuille Flow}
Consider Poiseuille flow in a cylinder with radius $ a $, bottom and top at $ z=0 $ and $ z=L $ with pressure $ p_0 + \Delta P $ at the bottom and $ p_0 $ at the top. This pressure gradient induces a flow upwards. We can ignore entrance and exist effects assuming that the cylinder is long and thin so $ L\gg a $.\par
In cylindrical polar coordinates, $ (r,\theta,z) $ Navier-Stokes equation becomes
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \frac 1r \frac{\partial p}{\partial \theta} = 0 &\\
	0 = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} + \mu \frac 1r \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left(r\frac{\partial w}{\partial r}\right) &\quad \text{for} \ \mathbf u= (0,0,w).
\end{align*}
So since $ \nabla\mathbf u = 0 \implies\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0\implies -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = G $ and we get
\[
	\implies \frac 1r \frac\partial{\partial r} r \frac{\partial w}{\partial r} = -\frac G\mu \implies\frac{\partial w}{\partial r} = -\frac {Gr}{2\mu} + \frac Ar
\]
and we get that $ A=0 $ since we have regularity at $ r=0 $ which is part of the flow. Now we get that
\[
	w = \frac G{4\mu}\left(a^2 - r^2\right)
\]
which gives our upwards flow, using the no-slip condition on the walls. Similarly we can calculate the flux,
\begin{align*}
	\text{Volumn Flux} = Q = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^a w \ \mathrm dr\mathrm d\theta &= 2\pi \frac{G}{4\mu} \left[\frac{a^2r^2}2 - \frac{r^4}4 \right]^a_0 \\
	&=\frac{\pi Ga^4}{8\mu}.
\end{align*}
We have that
\[
  \mathbf u =w \mathbf e_z \implies e = \begin{pmatrix}
	  0 & 0 & \frac 12 \frac{\partial w}{\partial r} \\
	  0 & 0 & 0 \\
	  \frac 12 \frac{\partial w}{\partial r} & 0 & 0
  \end{pmatrix},\quad \sigma_{rz} = 2\mu\sigma_{rz} = \mu\frac{\partial w}{\partial r} = -\frac {Gr}2.
\]
We can show that the total shear stress \textit{by} the fluid \textit{on} the tube is equal to the pressure force applied to the fluid which is $ \Delta p \pi a^2\mathbf e_z $. The total dissipation is equal to the rate of doing work (Example Sheet 1, Question 5).
\begin{example}
  We could extend this to flow in an annulus, with two fluids arranged concentrically, or as an ellipsoid.
\end{example}
\subsection{Pressure driven flow in a rectangular channel}
Consider a rectangle of length $ L $ of fluid with flow in the $ z $ direction with $ \mathbf u = w(x,y)\mathbf e_z $. Let the input channel be width $ 2a $ in the $ x $ direction and height $ 2b $ in the $ y $ direction. We want
\[
	\mu \nabla^2 w = \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = - G
\]
with $  w = 0 $ on $ x = \pm a $, $ y= \pm b $. Suppose that $ a\gg b $. Then we'd have our normal parallel plate flow in the limit
\[
	w = \frac {G}{2\mu}(b^2-y^2)
\]
which we'll use as our particular solution since it solves the Poisson equation and the boundary condition $ w=0 $ on $ y=\pm b $, we just need to add a complementy solution for the boundary conditions. So we need a $ w^{\mathrm c}(x,y) $ such that $ \nabla^2 w^{\mathrm c} = 0 $ which satisfies the boundary conditions. We need $ w^c = 0 $ at $ y=\pm b $ and $ w^c = -\frac G{2\mu}(b^2-y^2) $ on $ x = \pm a $.\par
Since Laplace's equation is homogeneous, we can use seperation of variables and Fourier series of the $ x $ boundary condition to get that
\[
	w^{\mathrm c} = \frac{Gb^2}{2\mu}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{4(-1)^n}{\lambda_n^3} \cos\frac{\lambda_n y}b\frac{\cosh\frac{\lambda_n x}b}{\cosh\frac{\lambda_n a}{b}}
\]
where we're scaling so our eigenvalues of the operator are $ \lambda_n = \left(n+\frac 12 \right)\pi $.\par
So the flux is
\begin{align*}
	Q = \int_{-b}^b \int_{-a}^a w \ \mathrm dx\mathrm dy &= \frac{4\Delta p}{3\mu L} ab^3\left(1 - \frac{6b}a \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\tanh\left(\lambda_n \frac ab\right)}{\lambda_n^5}\right) \\
							     &= 2a\cdot q_{\text{2D}}\left(1- \text{correction due to the sides}\right).
\end{align*}
\subsection{Implusively started flat plate}
Consider a fluid in $ y>0 $, initially at rest and set into motion by a rigid plate at $ y=0 $, which starts to move with velocity $ (U,0) $ at $ t=0 $. This results in unidirectional flow $ \mathbf u = (u(y,t),0,0) $ which obeys the equation
\[
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2},\quad u(0,t) = U
\]
for $ t>0 $, and $ u\to 0 $ as $ y\to \infty $. The only relevant lengthscale from the equation is $ \sqrt{\nu t} $. Our similarity solution is $ u = U\mathrm{erfc}(\eta) $ where $ \eta = \frac{y}{2\sqrt{\eta t}} $, and $ \mathrm{erfc}(\eta) = \frac 2{\sqrt \pi} \int_{\eta}e^\infty e^{-s^2} \ \mathrm ds $. The stress by the fluid on the plate is $ \mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -\frac{\mu U}{\sqrt{\pi \nu t}} $. The rate of working by the plate on the fluid is $ U\left(-\mu \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right) $ which must equal the rate of change of kinetic energy of the flow plus the rate of dissipation. \par
It's easiest to check from integrating the equation $ \rho u \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\right) = \rho u\left(\nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right) $ which implies that
\begin{align*}
	\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^\infty \frac 12 \rho u^2 \ \mathrm dy = \mu \int_0^\infty u \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\ \mathrm dy = \left[ \mu u \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right]^\infty_0 - \mu \int_0^\infty \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right)^2 \ \mathrm dy.
\end{align*}
\subsection{Oscillating flat plate}
Consider a fluid in $ y>0 $ with a rigid plate at $ y=0 $ which has velocity $ U\cos(\omega t) $ with vertical length scale $ \delta $. Then we have 
\[
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}
\]
again. We have by the no-slip condition that $ u = \mathrm{Re}(Ue^{i\omega t}) $ at $ y=0 $ and the boundary condition that $ u\to 0 $ as $ y\to\infty $. Steady oscillations (after decay of transients gives that
\begin{align*}
	u &=\mathrm{Re}\left(Ue^{i\omega t}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{i\omega}\nu}}y\right) \\
	  &= U e^{-ky} \cos(\omega t- ky)
\end{align*}
where $ k = \left(\frac{\omega}{2\nu}\right)^{\frac 12} $, so we get that
\[
	\delta \sim \frac 1k \sim \left(\frac {\nu}\omega\right)^{\frac 12} \sim (\nu T)^{\frac 12}.
\]
Again, the rate of working is given by the change in kinetic energy plus the rate of dissipation, but time dependency makes this complicated.\par
For the time-averaged energy calculations (quadratic quantities) note that $ \langle \cos^2\rangle = \langle \sin^2\rangle = \frac 12 $, $ \langle \cos\sin\rangle = 0 $, and
\[
	\langle \mathrm{Re}(Ae^{i\omega t})\mathrm{Re}(Be^{i\omega t})\rangle = \frac 12 \mathrm{Re}(AB^*).
\]
\subsection{Taylor-Couette Flow}
This is flow between long coaxial rotating cylinders. The flow is axisymmetric so $ \mathbf u = v(r) \mathbf e_\theta $. The inner cylinder $ r==a $ rotates with angular velocity $ \Omega_2 $ and the outer cylinder $ r=b $ rotates with angular velocity $ \Omega_1 $. We want Navier-Stokes equations in cylindrical polar coordinates. In fluids we need $ \nabla^2 \mathbf u $. We can use the identity that
\[
  \nabla^2 \mathbf u = \nabla(\nabla \cdot\mathbf u) - \nabla\times(\nabla\times \mathbf u)
\]
and the first term on the RHS is zero in an incompressible fluid which makes things much simplier. Formula are not required to be memorised for the exam. The case of Taylor-Couette flow however is sufficiently simple so we can do it by hand. We obtain that 
\[
	(\mathbf u \cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{v^2}r \mathbf e_r
\]
which is the formula for circular motion. There's no radial component of the viscous term so we get that
\[
	-\rho \frac{v^2} r= - \frac{\partial p}{\partial r}
\]
so the radial pressure gradient balances the centrifugal force. In the azimuthal direction (since $ \frac{\partial p}{\partial \theta} =0) $ we have that
\[
   0 = \mu\left(\frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial r^2} + \frac 1r\frac{\partial v}{\partial r} - \frac{v}{r^2}\right)
\]
with $ v(a_1) = \Omega_1a_1 $ and $ v(a_2) = \Omega_2 a_2 $. This is a Cauchy-Euler equation, which is solved with the boundary conditions (Example Sheet 1, Question 6).
\section{Stokes Flows}
Stokes flows are flows with $ \mathrm{Re}\ll 1 $, so we neglect inertial forces. Then Navier-Stokes equations become
\[
   0 =\mathbf f - \nabla p + \mu \nabla^2 \mathbf u,\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0.
\]
Recall that $ \mathrm{Re} = \frac{UL}{\nu} $ so this is good for $ U $ and $ L $ small (for example micro-organisms and aerosols) or if the viscosity is very large like lava flows or ice-sheets.\par
We will often assume that $ \mathbf f = \mathbf 0 $ as we can remove $ \rho\mathbf g $ from the equation by subtracting the hydrostatic pressure from the problem, $ p_0-\rho gz $ from $ p $. In this case we have that
\[
  \mathbf 0 = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2 \mathbf u = \nabla \cdot \sigma.
\]
\subsection{Simple Properties}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The flow is instanteous, so there is no $ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} $ term, so this is a pure spacial problem. The flow only depends on the boundary conditions (and $ \mathbf f $) at each instant. It may depend on time parametrically, in which we say the flow is quasi-steady if for example the boundary conditions are changing with time.
	\item The equation is linaer since there is no $ (\mathbf u \cdot\nabla)\mathbf u $ term. So $ \mathbf u, p, \sigma $ are linear in the boundary conditions (and in $ \mathbf f $).\par
\begin{example}
  Suppose we have some body in a fluid moving with velocity $ \mathbf U $ with a drag force equal to $ -A\cdot\mathbf U $ where $ A $ is a second rank tensor. We can use symmetric/anti-symmetric decomposition to break up a problem into two problems where one velocity is parallel with the axis of the body, and the other is perpendicular.
\end{example}
\item Changing the sign of all of the boundary conditions (and $\mathbf f $) results in just changing the sign of $ \mathbf u $. Reversing all the boundary conditions and the history of their application results in the fluid particles retracing their paths exactly.
\item If we have symmetry in space then due to linearity and reversibility we can use the symmetry of the geometry to show something is zero.
	\begin{example}
Consider a sphere falling under $ \mathbf g $ by a vertical wall. We'll show that the sphere must fall vertically. Take coordinates with $ z $ vertical and $ x $ perpendicular to the wall. Suppose not and the sphere falls non-vertically with a component in the $ x-y $ plane going away from the wall (wlog). Then reverse gravity so we reverse the velocity $ \mathbf U $. The problem is symmetrical so we can reflect the problem along the sphere's equator, perpendicular to the wall. So we can $ \mathbf g $ acting down again and $ \mathbf U $ pointing towards the wall. But by symmetry it can't have a component both towards the wall and away from the wall. So $ U_x = 0 $ and similarly by a symmetric argument $ U_y = 0 $. 
	\end{example}
	We'll show a similar result with rotation (psuedovectors) instead of velocity.
	\begin{example}
		(Falling uniform rod does not rotate). Suppose a rod falls with angular velocity $ \Omega $ due to the falling motion. Reverse $ g $ so $ \Omega $ now goes the other way and $ g $ points up. Now rotate by $ \pi $ so we have the same problem with $ \Omega $ going the opposite direction, so by symmetry we have that $ \Omega = 0 $, i.e there is no rotation induced by the fall.
	\end{example}
\item We have harmonic properties (for $ \mathbf f = \mathbf 0) $, so
	\[
	  \mu\nabla^2 \mathbf u = \nabla p,\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0
	\]
	so taking divergence of the left equation we get that $ p $ is harmoinc. Taking the curl of the left equation we get $ \nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega = 0 $ so vorticity is harmonic. From taking the Laplacian of the left equation we get that $ \nabla^2\nabla^2 \mathbf u = \nabla^4 \mathbf u = 0 $ which we say that $ \mathbf u $ is biharmonic.
\end{enumerate}
	\subsection{More Properties}
	We'll first assume that $ \mathbf f = \mathbf 0 $ so we have that $ \nabla \cdot \sigma = 0 $.\par
We have uniqueness of flows. Suppose $ \mathbf u_1 $ and $ \mathbf u_2 $ are Stokes flows in a domain $ V $ and satisfy the same boundary conditions on $ \partial V $. Let $ \mathbf u = \mathbf u_1 - \mathbf u_2 $ and $ e = e_1-e_2 $. Then we get that
		\begin{align*}
			D = 2\mu \int_V e_{ij}e_{ij}\ \mathrm dV &= \int (\sigma_{ij} +  p\delta_{ij})e_{ij}\ \mathrm dV \\
								 &= \int_V \sigma_{ij}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\ \mathrm dV \\ 
								 &= \int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}(u_i\sigma_{ij}) - u_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\sigma_{ij}\ \mathrm dV \\
								 &= \int_{\partial V} \mathbf u \cdot \sigma \cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS \\
								 &= 0
		\end{align*}
		since $ \mathbf u_1 = \mathbf u_2 $ or the stress equals on the boundary ($ \sigma_1\cdot\mathbf n =\sigma_2\cdot\mathbf n $ on $ \partial V $. So we get $ \mathbf e = 0 $ in $ V $. So $ \mathbf u $ is at most a solid body motion. If $ \mathbf u_1 = \mathbf u_2 $ on $ \partial V $ them $ \mathbf u_1 = \mathbf u_2 $ in $ V $. Instead if $ \sigma_1\cdot\mathbf n = \sigma_1\cdot\mathbf n $ then $ \mathbf u_1 = \mathbf u_2 +\mathbf U + \boldsymbol\Omega \times \mathbf x $.
\begin{theorem}
	(Minimum dissipation theorem) Let $ \mathbf u^{\mathbf S} $ be a Stokes flow in $ V $ and $ \mathbf u $ be any incompressible flow that has the same velocity on $ \partial V $. Then
	\[
		2\mu \int_V e_{ij}^{\mathrm S} e_{ij}^{\mathrm S}\ \mathrm dV \le 2\mu \int_V e_{ij}e_{ij}\ \mathrm dV
	\]
\end{theorem}
\pf We have that
\begin{align*}
	0 &\le 2\mu \int_V (e_{ij}-e^{\mathrm S}_{ij})(e_{ij} -e_{ij}^{\mathrm S}) \ \mathrm dV \\
	  &= 2\mu \int_V (e_{ij}e_{ij} - e^{\mathrm S}_{ij}e^{\mathrm S}_{ij}) \ \mathrm dV + 4\mu \int_V e^{\mathrm S}_{ij}(e^{\mathrm S}_{ij} - e_{ij})\ \mathrm dV.
\end{align*}
The last term (using incompressibility in both flows) is equal to
\begin{align*}
	2\int_V \sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm S}(e_{ij}^{\mathrm S} - e_{ij}) &= 2\int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}((u_i^{\mathrm S} - u_i) \sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm S}) - (u_i^{\mathrm S} - u_i)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\sigma_{ij}^{\mathrm S} \ \mathrm dV \\
								     &= 2\int_{\partial V} (u_i^{\mathrm S} - u_i)\sigma_{ij}n_j \ \mathrm dS = 0
\end{align*}
so we get the required result.\qed
\begin{example}
	We can bound drag on a translating rigid body. Consider an arbirary rigid body, translating with velocity $ \mathbf U $ with Stokes flow $ \mathbf u^{\mathrm S} $ in the fluid volume $ V $ around it. Take $ \mathbf n $ into the boundy so 
	\begin{align*}
		\int_{\partial V} \sigma\cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS&= \text{force } F_{\mathrm S} \text{ exerted by body on fluid}
	\end{align*}
 we have that the dissipation
	\begin{align*}
		D &= 2\mu \int_V e^{\mathrm S}_{ij}e^{\mathrm S}_{ij} \ \mathrm dV \\
		  &= \int_{\partial V} \mathbf u^{\mathrm S} \cdot\sigma\cdot\mathbf n  \ \mathrm dS = \mathbf U \cdot \int_{\partial V} \sigma^{\mathrm S}\cdot \mathbf n \ \mathrm dS = \mathbf U \cdot \mathbf F^{\mathrm S}.
	\end{align*}
	Now suppose that $ S $ is a sphere of radius $ a $ which circumscribes the body. Define a flow $ \mathbf u $ in the original fluid volume $ V $ by taking the Stokes flow outside the rigid sphere $ S $ moving the velocity $ \mathbf U $ and taking uniform flow $ \mathbf U $ in the gap between $ S $ and the body. So we get that
	\[
		2\mu \int_V e_{ij}e_{ij} \ \mathrm dV = 2\mu \int_{\text{outside of }} S e_{ij}e_{ij} \ \mathrm dV
	\]
	since $ e_{ij} = 0 $ in the gap. Now this is equal to
	\[
		\mathbf U \cdot (\text{force exerted by sphere})
	\]
	which is $ 6\pi \mu a \mathbf U $ (result derivation later). Hence the minimium dissipation gives that
	\[
		\mathbf U \cdot\mathbf F^{\mathrm S} \le 6\pi \mu aU^2.
	\]

\end{example}
We have a similar argument for an inscribed sphere of radius $ b $ so we get the same formula but with $ b $ instead of $ a $. Then for a cube of size $ 2L $ set $ b =L $ and $ a =\sqrt 3 L $ so from the symmetries of the cube with $ \mathbf F^{\mathrm S} = A\mathbf U $ has the same $ A $ for all orientations (Example Sheet 2, Question 2) and we deduce that
\[
	1 \le \frac A{6\pi \mu L}\le \sqrt 3
\]
and we have a regular argument for couple and rotation.\par
\begin{theorem}
	(Reciprocal Theorem) Let $ \mathbf u^1 $ and $ \mathbf u^2 $ be Stokes flows in some domain $ V $ with different boundary conditions on $ \partial V $. Then
	\[
		\int_{\partial V}\mathbf u^1 \cdot\sigma^2 \cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS = \int_{\partial V}\mathbf u^2 \cdot\sigma^1\cdot\mathbf n\ \mathrm dS.
	\]
\end{theorem}
\pf We'll go over a quick outline of the proof now. Consider
\begin{align*}
 2\mu \int_V e_{ij}^2e_{ij}^2 \ \mathrm dV &= \int_V(\sigma^1_{ij} + p^1\delta_{ij})e_{ij}^2 \ \mathrm V
\end{align*}
and we can get rid of the pressure term since $ \nabla \cdot\mathbf u^2 = 0 $, and using the fact that $ \sigma^1_{ij} = \sigma^2_{ji} $, so we have that (after applying integration by parts and using the fact that the divergence of $ \sigma^1 $ is zero and using the divergence theorem)
\[
	\cdots = \int_{\partial V} \mathbf u^2 \cdot\sigma^1 \cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS = RHS
\]
and since the first line was symmetric, the theorem follows. \qed
\begin{example}
	Suppose that $ \mathbf u^{1,2} $ are Stokes flows due to rigid body motion of a certain body (some $ V $) i.e that $ \mathbf u^{1,2} = \mathbf U^{1,2} +\boldsymbol \Omega^{1,2} \times \mathbf x $ on $ \partial V $ where $ \mathbf U^i,\boldsymbol\Omega^i $ are constants. The reciprocal theorem gives that
	\begin{equation}
		\mathbf U^1 \cdot\mathbf F^2 +\boldsymbol\Omega^1 \cdot\mathbf G^2 = \mathbf U^2 \cdot\mathbf F^1 + \boldsymbol\Omega^2 \cdot\mathbf G^1\tag{*} 
	\end{equation}
	where 
	\[
		\mathbf F = \int_{\partial V} \sigma \cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS\quad \text{and}\quad \mathbf G = \int_{\partial V}\mathbf x \times \sigma \cdot\mathbf n \ \mathrm dS
	\]
	are the corresponding forces and couples exerted by/on the body $ \mathbf n $ out/in of the fluid.
	\par
	By the linearity of the Stokes equations we have that 
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbf F &= A\cdot\mathbf U + B\cdot\boldsymbol \Omega \\
		\mathbf G &= C\cdot\mathbf U + D\cdot\boldsymbol \Omega
	\end{align*}
	where the tensors $ A,B,C,D $ depend on the shape of the body. We can show that using $ (*) $ that $ A=A^\top, D=D^\top $ and $ B= C^\top $.\par
	For a sphere and a cube, symmetries imply that
	\[
	  A = AI, D=DI, B=C^\top = 0
	\]
	where the $ LHS $ is a tensor and the $ RHS $ is a constant multiplied by the identity tensor.\par
	For a helix $ B = C^\top \ne 0 $.
\end{example}
\subsection{Flow Past a Sphere}
\subsubsection{Stationary Sphere in Uniform Flow}
Consider a sphere with radius $ a $ with a uniform flow $ \mathbf U $ past a fixed rigid sphere, radius $ a $. There are several methods, all which have heavy algebra somewhere or depend on heavy usage of spherical polar coordinates.
\par
The solution is
\[
	\mathbf u =\mathbf U \left(1 - \frac{3a}{4r} - \frac{a^3}{4r^3}\right) + \mathbf x(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x) \left(-\frac {3a}{4r^3} + \frac {3a^3}{4r^5}\right)
\]

which we can derive in many painful ways.
%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Method 1 (semi-modern, linearity and vector method)}

From the linearity of the Stokes equations, $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})$ must be linear in $\mathbf{U}$. Taking the origin to be at the centre of the sphere, the spherically symmetric geometry means the only other vector ingredient for $\mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{x}$. Hence the velocity and pressure fields must take the forms
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbf{U} f(r) + \mathbf{x}(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\, g(r), \\
  p(\mathbf{x}) &= \mu (\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\, h(r),
\end{align*}
where $r = |\mathbf{x}|$, and $f$, $g$ and $h$ are scalar functions of $r$ to be determined.

Differentiate
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}
  = U_i x_j f'/r + \delta_{ij} U_n x_n g + x_i U_j g + x_i x_j U_n x_n\, g'/r.
  \tag{$*$}
\end{equation}
Contract $i$ with $j$ and apply incompressibility:
\[
  \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = U_n x_n \bigl( f'/r + 4g + r g' \bigr) = 0.
\]
Differentiate again:
\begin{align*}
  \mu\nabla^2 u_i &= \mu U_i \bigl( f'' + 2f'/r + 2g \bigr) + \mu x_i U_n x_n \bigl( g'' + 6g'/r \bigr), \\
  \nabla_i p &= \mu U_i h + \mu x_i U_n x_n\, h'/r.
\end{align*}
In $\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} = \nabla p$ equate the bits with $U_i$ and those with $x_i U_n x_n$. So the Stokes equations give
\[
  f'/r + 4g + r g' = 0, \qquad f'' + 2f'/r + 2g = h \qquad\text{and}\qquad g'' + 6g'/r = h'/r.
\]
Eliminating $h$ and then $f$ yields
\[
  r^2 g''' + 11 r g'' + 24 g' = 0.
\]
This ODE is equidimensional in $r$ so look for solutions of the form $g = r^\alpha$. Substituting, we find $\alpha = 0, -3$ and $-5$, with associated $f = -(\alpha+4)\, r^{\alpha+2}/(\alpha+2)$ and $h = -(\alpha+5)(\alpha+2)\, r^\alpha$.

Hence the general solution of the assumed form linear in $\mathbf{U}$ is
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}) &= \mathbf{U}\Bigl( -2Ar^2 + B + Cr^{-1} - \tfrac{1}{3} D r^{-3} \Bigr)
    + \mathbf{x}(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\Bigl( A + Cr^{-3} + Dr^{-5} \Bigr), \\
  p(\mathbf{x}) &= \mu(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\Bigl( -10A + 2Cr^{-3} \Bigr).
\end{align*}

Find $\mathbf{e}$ from $(*)$ and thus $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$: the stress across a spherical surface with normal $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{x}/r$ is
\[
  \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}
  = \mu\mathbf{U}\Bigl( -3Ar + 2Dr^{-4} \Bigr)
  + \mu\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\Bigl( 9Ar^{-1} - 6Cr^{-4} - 6Dr^{-6} \Bigr).
\]

Applying the boundary conditions $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$ on the rigid sphere and $\mathbf{u} \to \mathbf{U}$ in the far field, we find the coefficients
\[
  A = 0, \qquad B = 1, \qquad C = -\tfrac{3}{4}a \qquad\text{and}\qquad D = \tfrac{3}{4}a^3,
\]
so
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{u} &= \mathbf{U}\left( 1 - \frac{3a}{4r} - \frac{a^3}{4r^3} \right)
    + \mathbf{x}(\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x})\left( -\frac{3a}{4r^3} + \frac{3a^3}{4r^5} \right), \\
  p &= -\frac{3a\mu\, \mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{2r^3}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}\Big|_{r=a} = \frac{3\mu}{2a}\mathbf{U}.
\end{align*}
Hence the drag on the sphere is
\[
  \int_{r=a} \boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}\, \mathrm{d}S
  = 4\pi a^2 \,\frac{3\mu}{2a}\mathbf{U} = 6\pi\mu a\,\mathbf{U}.
\]

\subsubsection{Method 2 (classical, separable streamfunction in sphericals $r, \theta, \phi$)}

Use a Stokes streamfunction $\Psi(r,\theta)$ for the axisymmetric flow with
\[
  u_r = \frac{1}{r^2 \sin\theta}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\theta}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  u_\theta = -\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial r}
\]
(from $\mathbf{u} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{A} = \Psi\,\mathbf{e}_\phi / r\sin\theta$ so that $\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\Psi = 0$). In Stokes flow $\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow \nabla^4\mathbf{A} = (\nabla\times)^4\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{0}$ and (with patience or Wikipedia)
\[
  D^2 D^2 \Psi = 0
  \qquad\text{where}\qquad
  D^2 = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left( \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \right).
\]
The uniform flow at infinity (the nonzero condition) has $\Psi = \tfrac{1}{2}Ur^2\sin^2\theta$; try $\Psi = F(r)\sin^2\theta$ and find $F = Ar^4 + Br^2 + Cr + D/r$. Then $A = 0$, $B = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and apply the BCs on sphere.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Method 3 (semi-classical, hybrid)}

The vorticity is a harmonic function (as is the pressure), decaying at infinity, linear in $\mathbf{U}$ and otherwise constructed only from $\mathbf{x}$ by spherical symmetry. Can be shown the decaying solutions of $\nabla^2(\cdot) = 0$ are $\dfrac{1}{r}$, $\nabla\dfrac{1}{r} = -\mathbf{x}/r^3$, $\nabla\nabla\dfrac{1}{r}$, etc. Hence the vorticity, a pseudo vector, (and the pressure, a scalar) must have the form
\[
  \boldsymbol{\omega} = A\,\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{x}/r^3
  \qquad\bigl(\text{and } p = \mu B\,\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{x}/r^3\bigr).
\]
Return to spherical polars and solve $D^2\Psi = AU\sin^2\theta / r$ with $\Psi = F(r)\sin^2\theta$. From $\nabla p = -\mu\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\omega}$ get $B = \mu A$.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Method 4 (modern, Papkovich--Neuber)}

If $\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})$ is a vector harmonic function ($\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\phi} = \mathbf{0}$) then
\[
  \mathbf{u} = \nabla(\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}) - 2\boldsymbol{\phi},
  \qquad
  p = 2\mu\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}.
\]
is a solution of the Stokes equations (Sheet 2, Q5). It's not quite the general solution (see Part III), but does include this problem.

Linearity and spherical symmetry give (for the perturbation $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{U}$, which decays at infinity)
\[
  \boldsymbol{\phi} = A\,\mathbf{U}\,\frac{1}{r} + B\,\mathbf{U}\cdot\nabla\nabla\frac{1}{r},
\]
with coefficients to be determined by applying the boundary conditions.

\begin{remark}

Velocity is a true vector. Vorticity/angular velocity are pseudo vectors. (Difference is behaviour under reflections.) Sometimes linearity is not sufficient, you need to pay attention to the true vs.\ pseudo nature of the vectors involved. E.g.\ rotation of a sphere: $\mathbf{u}$ (true vector) linear in $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ (pseudo vector) gives (by Method 1)
\[
  \mathbf{u} = \boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{x}\, f(r).
\]
\end{remark}

Often deal with a sphere moving with velocity $\mathbf{U}$ in fluid otherwise at rest. Remove uniform flow from the above, and put $\mathbf{U} \to -\mathbf{U}$. Better, start from scratch with correct BCs. Can also use general algebra ($A$, $B$, $C$, $D$) with different BCs for a spherical bubble (Example Sheet 2, Question 6), or a spherical drop.
\end{remark}
